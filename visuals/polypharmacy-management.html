💊 Polypharmacy Management in Older Adults 💊

Systematic Approach for D120

Polypharmacy Definition

Use of 5 or more medications concurrently

Common in older adults (>40% of adults age 65+ take 5+ medications)

Risks: Drug interactions, adverse effects, falls, cognitive impairment, medication non-adherence, increased hospitalizations

Medication Review Flowchart

1

Complete Medication Inventory

Brown Bag Method: Ask patient to bring ALL medications

  • Prescription medications
  • Over-the-counter drugs
  • Vitamins, supplements, herbals
  • Topical medications, eye drops, inhalers
  • PRN medications

Document: Name, dose, frequency, indication, prescriber, start date

Assess: What patient is ACTUALLY taking vs. what's prescribed

2

Review Indication for Each Medication

  • Is the indication valid? Does patient still need this medication?
  • Is it evidence-based? Does it align with current guidelines?
  • Expired indications: Short-term medications continued long-term (PPIs, sleep aids)
  • Duplications: Multiple medications from same class
  • Medication without clear indication: Prescribing cascade (treating side effects of other drugs)

Action: Discontinue medications without valid indication

3

Apply Beers Criteria & STOPP/START

Beers Criteria: Potentially inappropriate medications (PIMs) in older adults

STOPP: Screening Tool of Older Persons' Prescriptions (what to stop)

START: Screening Tool to Alert to Right Treatment (what to start)

See detailed lists below ⬇

4

Assess Drug-Drug Interactions

  • Use interaction checker (Lexicomp, Micromedex, Epocrates)
  • High-risk combinations:
    • Warfarin + NSAIDs (bleeding risk)
    • SSRIs + NSAIDs (GI bleeding)
    • Multiple QT-prolonging drugs
    • Multiple anticholinergic drugs (cognitive impairment)
    • Multiple CNS depressants (falls, sedation)
  • Consider pharmacokinetic interactions (CYP450 metabolism)
5

Evaluate Dose Appropriateness

  • Renal function: Calculate CrCl (Cockcroft-Gault), adjust renally-cleared drugs
  • Hepatic function: Adjust if liver disease
  • Age-related changes: ↓ metabolism, ↑ sensitivity in older adults
  • "Start low, go slow" principle in geriatrics
  • Common medications requiring renal adjustment:
    • Gabapentin, pregabalin
    • Metformin (hold if CrCl <30)
    • Antibiotics (many)
    • Digoxin
6

Deprescribe When Appropriate

  • Prioritize: Medications with highest risk/lowest benefit
  • Taper when necessary: Benzodiazepines, SSRIs, PPIs, opioids, beta-blockers
  • One at a time: Easier to identify effects of changes
  • Patient involvement: Shared decision-making, explain rationale
  • Monitor: Follow-up for withdrawal effects, symptom recurrence
7

Optimize Administration & Adherence

  • Simplify regimen: Once-daily dosing when possible, reduce pill burden
  • Address adherence barriers: Cost, complexity, side effects, cognitive impairment
  • Tools: Pill organizers, medication lists, alarms/reminders
  • Caregiver involvement: Medication management support
  • Pharmacy coordination: Single pharmacy, medication synchronization
8

Schedule Regular Medication Reviews

  • Frequency: At least annually, more often if changes or issues
  • Triggers for review: Hospitalization, new diagnosis, functional decline, falls
  • Document: Medication list updates, changes made, rationale
  • Communicate: With all providers, patient, caregivers

⚠️ Beers Criteria: Potentially Inappropriate Medications (PIMs) in Older Adults

American Geriatrics Society - Updated 2023

Anticholinergics

  • First-gen antihistamines (diphenhydramine, hydroxyzine)
  • Tricyclic antidepressants
  • Antispasmodics (oxybutynin, tolterodine)

Why avoid: Cognitive impairment, confusion, constipation, urinary retention, falls

Benzodiazepines

  • All benzodiazepines
  • Non-benzo hypnotics (zolpidem, eszopiclone)

Why avoid: ↑ Fall risk, cognitive impairment, delirium, dependence

NSAIDs

  • Non-selective NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen)
  • COX-2 inhibitors (celecoxib)
  • Aspirin >325 mg/day

Why avoid: GI bleeding, renal impairment, ↑ BP, heart failure exacerbation

PPIs

  • Long-term use (>8 weeks) without clear indication

Why avoid: C. diff, fracture risk, nutrient malabsorption (B12, Mg), dementia risk (controversial)

Antipsychotics

  • Especially in dementia (black box warning)
  • First-generation (haloperidol) and second-generation

Why avoid: ↑ Stroke, mortality in dementia; falls, sedation, anticholinergic effects

Sliding Scale Insulin

  • Alone without basal insulin

Why avoid: ↑ Hypoglycemia risk, not evidence-based

Sulfonylureas (long-acting)

  • Glyburide, chlorpropamide

Why avoid: Prolonged hypoglycemia risk

Muscle Relaxants

  • Cyclobenzaprine, methocarbamol

Why avoid: Anticholinergic effects, sedation, limited efficacy in elderly

Digoxin

  • Dose >0.125 mg/day

Why avoid: ↓ Renal clearance → toxicity risk

🛑 STOPP - Medications to STOP

  • PPIs for >8 weeks without clear indication
  • Benzodiazepines >4 weeks
  • Anticholinergics in patients with dementia
  • NSAIDs in heart failure, CKD, or with anticoagulants
  • Aspirin without cardiovascular indication
  • Antipsychotics as first-line for behavioral symptoms of dementia
  • Duplicate drug classes
  • Medications treating side effects of another drug (prescribing cascade)

✅ START - Medications to START (if indicated)

  • Statin in diabetes + cardiovascular risk factors
  • ACE-I/ARB in heart failure with reduced EF or diabetic nephropathy
  • Beta-blocker post-MI
  • Calcium + Vitamin D for osteoporosis prevention
  • Bisphosphonate in osteoporosis with fracture history
  • Anticoagulation in atrial fibrillation (appropriate CHA₂DS₂-VASc score)
  • Laxative in chronic opioid use

Common Adverse Drug Events in Older Adults

Adverse Event High-Risk Medications Prevention/Management
Falls Benzodiazepines, sedatives, antipsychotics, opioids, antihypertensives, anticholinergics Taper/discontinue if possible; fall risk assessment; PT/OT referral
Delirium Anticholinergics, benzodiazepines, opioids, antihistamines Avoid in high-risk patients; use lowest effective dose; non-pharm alternatives
Cognitive Impairment Anticholinergics (cumulative effect), benzodiazepines Calculate anticholinergic burden; deprescribe when possible
GI Bleeding NSAIDs + anticoagulants/antiplatelets; NSAIDs + SSRIs Avoid combination; use PPI if NSAID necessary; consider acetaminophen alternative
Acute Kidney Injury NSAIDs, ACE-I/ARB + diuretic ("triple whammy"), metformin Monitor renal function; adjust doses; hydration during acute illness
Hypoglycemia Sulfonylureas (especially glyburide), insulin Use safer alternatives (DPP-4i, SGLT2i); individualize A1C goals (7.5-8% acceptable in frail elderly)
Orthostatic Hypotension Alpha-blockers, antihypertensives, diuretics, vasodilators Check orthostatic BP; slow titration; educate on position changes
Urinary Retention Anticholinergics, opioids, antihistamines Especially in men with BPH; avoid anticholinergics; use alternatives

🚨 High-Risk Medication Situations

  • Prescribing cascade: Adding medication to treat side effect of another drug (e.g., metoclopramide for NSAID-induced nausea)
  • Anticholinergic burden: Cumulative effect of multiple medications with anticholinergic properties → cognitive impairment
  • "Triple whammy": NSAID + ACE-I/ARB + diuretic → acute kidney injury
  • Serotonin syndrome: Multiple serotonergic drugs (SSRI + tramadol, SSRI + linezolid)
  • QT prolongation: Multiple QT-prolonging drugs + electrolyte abnormalities
  • Warfarin interactions: Many drug/food interactions; requires close monitoring

💡 Clinical Pearls for D120:

  • "Start low, go slow" but "don't go too low" - under-dosing can be as harmful as over-dosing
  • Deprescribing is active treatment, not neglect - requires monitoring and follow-up
  • Calculate CrCl (not just serum creatinine) to assess renal function in elderly - use Cockcroft-Gault
  • Beers criteria is a guide, not absolute - consider individual patient context
  • Medication review at every visit, especially after hospitalization (medication reconciliation)
  • OTC doesn't mean safe - many OTC drugs (NSAIDs, diphenhydramine) are high-risk in elderly
  • Supplements count - herbal products can interact with medications (St. John's wort, ginkgo)
  • Simplify when possible: Once-daily formulations, combination pills, reduce total number
  • Patient goals matter: In advanced illness, prioritize quality of life over preventive meds with long-term benefit
  • Team approach: Involve pharmacist in medication review - they're the experts!
