## 🎯 Contraception: Choosing the Right Method

### Why This Matters
Maria, 24, comes in saying she 'doesn't want to be on the pill' because it made her gain weight and feel moody. Her sister just had an IUD placed and loves it, but Maria heard 'it can perforate your uterus.' She's sexually active with one partner and wants something 'set it and forget it.' Every day in primary care, you'll help patients navigate these choices — and getting it right means preventing unintended pregnancy AND keeping them coming back for care.

[Visual: Decision tree showing patient preferences branching to different contraceptive options with efficacy rates]

### Contraceptive Efficacy: The Tier System
**CDC Medical Eligibility Criteria (MEC)** categorizes methods by **typical-use efficacy**:

**Tier 1 — Most Effective (>99%)**:
• **LARC (Long-Acting Reversible Contraception)** — IUDs (Mirena, Paragard, Kyleena, Liletta) and implant (Nexplanon)
• **Sterilization** — tubal ligation, vasectomy
• Failure rate: <1 per 100 woman-years

**Tier 2 — Effective (91-94%)**:
• **Oral contraceptive pills** (COCs + POPs)
• **Contraceptive patch** (Xulane, Twirla)
• **Vaginal ring** (NuvaRing, Annovera)
• **DMPA injection** (Depo-Provera)

**Tier 3 — Less Effective (72-88%)**:
• **Condoms** (male + female)
• **Diaphragm**, **cervical cap**, **sponge**
• **Withdrawal**, **rhythm method**

**Key Point**: LARC has HIGHEST satisfaction and continuation rates. ACOG recommends LARC as first-line for MOST adolescents and women.

[Visual: Pyramid graphic with tiers — Tier 1 (LARC/sterilization) at top with >99%, Tier 2 (pills/patch/ring/DMPA) with 91-94%, Tier 3 (condoms/barrier) with 72-88%]

**Memory Trick:** **LARC First**: **L**ong-**A**cting **R**eversible **C**ontraception = **L**owest failure, **A**ll ages, **R**eally effective, **C**ost-effective over time

🔥 **LARC myths debunked**: IUDs do NOT cause infertility, pelvic infection (except at insertion if active STI), or perforation (risk 1/1000, usually at insertion). Nexplanon insertion/removal is faster than a Pap smear. **Mirena is FDA-approved for heavy menstrual bleeding** — a two-for-one win in perimenopausal patients.

### Quick-Start Contraception Protocol
**Quick-Start** — start hormones the day of visit (no need to wait for menses) IF:
• Reasonably certain not pregnant (no recent unprotected sex, no signs of pregnancy)
• **Urine pregnancy test negative** (obtain first!)
• If started >5 days after menses onset → use backup (condoms) for 7 days

**IUD Insertion Checklist**:
✅ CBC if heavy menses
✅ **Chlamydia/gonorrhea screening** (treat before insertion if positive)
✅ Bimanual exam to determine uterine position
✅ Insert during menses for easier cervix (optional, can do anytime)
✅ Counsel on expected cramping/spotting for 1-3 months
✅ Ultrasound guidance if difficult insertion or uncertain position

**When to REFER**: Uterine anomaly, recent postpartum <4 weeks, active PID, cervical or endometrial cancer

[Visual: Flowchart: Patient wants contraception → Pregnancy test → Negative → Assess preferences → Choose method → If LARC: STI screening → Insert → Follow-up in 4-6 weeks]

**Q:** Which contraceptive method has the HIGHEST typical-use efficacy and continuation rate?
  [ ] Oral contraceptive pills
  [ ] Depo-Provera injection
  [✓] LARC (IUD/implant)
  [ ] Male condoms
  *LARC methods have >99% efficacy and highest satisfaction + continuation rates per ACOG. Pills have 91% typical use and high discontinuation due to daily adherence.*

**Q:** A 19-year-old wants IUD placement. What MUST you do first?
  [ ] Order pelvic ultrasound
  [✓] Screen for chlamydia and gonorrhea
  [ ] Start antibiotics prophylactically
  [ ] Wait until she has had a child
  *Screen for STIs before IUD insertion. If positive, treat first. Prophylactic antibiotics are NOT recommended. Nulliparity is NOT a contraindication to IUDs.*

## 🔥 Menopause: Navigating the Transition

### Why This Matters
Susan, 52, hasn't slept through the night in 6 months. She wakes up drenched in sweat, her mood is unpredictable ('my family walks on eggshells'), and she's gaining weight around her middle despite no change in diet. Her mother had a 'really bad menopause' and Susan says, 'I guess I just have to suffer through it like she did.' She doesn't — and neither do your patients. Menopause management is one of the most rewarding parts of primary care.

[Visual: Illustration of a woman with heat symbols around her head and chest, moon/crescent for disrupted sleep, and thought bubbles showing frustration and confusion]

### The Menopause Transition: STRAW+10 Staging
**STRAW+10 Staging System** (the standard):

• **Late Reproductive Stage**: Regular cycles, subtle FSH rise, declining fertility
• **Early Menopause Transition**: Variable cycle length (≥7 days difference from normal), elevated FSH
• **Late Menopause Transition**: ≥2 skipped cycles AND ≥1 interval of amenorrhea ≥60 days, FSH >25 IU/L
• **Early Menopause**: 12 months of amenorrhea (no other cause), FSH >40 IU/L
• **Late Menopause**: Begins 5 years after final menstrual period — urogenital symptoms dominant

**Perimenopause** = the transition period + first year after final period (3-8 years total)

**Key Symptoms**: Vasomotor symptoms (hot flashes, night sweats) — affect 75% of women. Sleep disturbance, mood changes, vaginal dryness, joint pain, brain fog.

[Visual: Timeline graphic from late reproductive through late menopause with cycle patterns, FSH levels, and symptom prevalence shown along the continuum]

**Memory Trick:** **STRAW Stick**: **S**tage the transition: **T**racking cycles → **R**ising FSH → **A**menorrhea 60+ days → **W**hole year no period

### Hormone Therapy: When, How, and For Whom
**Menopausal Hormone Therapy (MHT) — Indications**:
• Moderate-to-severe vasomotor symptoms (hot flashes, night sweats)
• Prevention of bone loss (in patients at high risk who can't take bisphosphonates)
• Genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM) — vaginal estrogen is FIRST-LINE

**The 'Window of Opportunity'**: Start MHT within **10 years of menopause** or age <60 for best risk-benefit ratio

**Regimens**:
| Patient Type | Regimen | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **With uterus** | Estrogen + Progestogen (protects endometrium) | Estradiol 0.5-1mg + micronized progesterone 100-200mg |
| **Without uterus** | Estrogen alone | Estradiol 0.5-1mg daily |
| **Vaginal symptoms only** | Low-dose vaginal estrogen | Estradiol vaginal cream/ring/tablet — minimal systemic absorption |

**Contraindications to MHT**:
❌ Breast cancer (current or history)
❌ Endometrial cancer
❌ Unexplained vaginal bleeding
❌ Active VTE or liver disease
❌ H/o coronary artery disease (if starting at age >60)

**Non-Hormonal Options for Hot Flashes**:
• **SSRI/SNRI**: Paroxetine 7.5mg (Brisdelle — FDA-approved!), venlafaxine 37.5-75mg
• **Gabapentin** 300-900mg (especially for nighttime hot flashes)
• **Oxybutynin** 2.5-5mg (emerging evidence)
• **Cognitive behavioral therapy** — reduces hot flash bother by 50%

[Visual: Decision matrix: Patient with vasomotor symptoms → Age/symptom severity → Uterus (yes/no) → Choose regimen → Reassess at 3 months then annually]

🔥 **Vaginal estrogen is SAFE** — minimal systemic absorption, does NOT require progestogen protection, and is safe even for breast cancer survivors (though discuss with oncologist). Don't deny this to women with only urogenital symptoms just because they 'can't take systemic hormones.' Also: **Testosterone is NOT FDA-approved for low libido in women** — off-label use requires counseling on risks (acne, hirsutism, voice changes) and monitoring labs.

### Perimenopause Workup
**When to work up symptoms before attributing to menopause**:

✅ **TSH** — thyroid disease mimics menopause (fatigue, weight gain, mood changes)
✅ **FSH** (not diagnostic alone — fluctuates! But helpful if >25 in transition)
✅ **Pregnancy test** — always! Amenorrhea ≠ menopause until proven
✅ **CBC, iron panel** — heavy menstrual bleeding can cause iron deficiency
✅ **Lipid panel, glucose** — CV risk increases after menopause (estrogen withdrawal)
✅ **Bone density (DXA)** at menopause if risk factors: smoking, family h/o osteoporosis, BMI <20, steroid use
✅ **Mammogram** — up to date before starting MHT

[Visual: Checklist-style infographic with a stethoscope, showing all screening items to rule out mimics before attributing symptoms to menopause]

**Q:** A 54-year-old woman with an intact uterus wants MHT for severe hot flashes. What's the BEST regimen?
  [ ] Estrogen alone
  [✓] Estrogen + progestogen
  [ ] Progestogen alone
  [ ] Testosterone + estrogen
  *Women with a uterus NEED progestogen to prevent endometrial hyperplasia/cancer from unopposed estrogen. Estrogen-only is for women s/p hysterectomy.*

**Q:** A 62-year-old breast cancer survivor has vaginal dryness and dyspareunia. What's the BEST option?
  [ ] Systemic estrogen therapy
  [✓] Vaginal estrogen cream
  [ ] Testosterone therapy
  [ ] No treatment available
  *Vaginal estrogen has minimal systemic absorption. It's considered safe in most breast cancer survivors (though discuss with oncologist). Systemic estrogen is contraindicated in hormone-positive breast cancer.*

## 🔬 Vaginitis & Abnormal Pap: From Symptoms to Management

### Why This Matters
Jasmine, 28, comes in embarrassed: 'I have this fishy smell and it's worse after sex. My partner says I'm fine but I'm so self-conscious.' She's tried OTC yeast treatments (Monistat × 3) with no improvement. She's worried something is 'seriously wrong.' Differentiating vaginitis types by history and office microscopy is a core FNP skill — treat the wrong thing and you make patients miserable (and waste their money on unnecessary OTC products).

[Visual: Microscopy comparison images: clue cells (BV), pseudohyphae (yeast), trichomonads (trich) side by side with labeled features]

### The Big Three: BV, Candidiasis, Trichomoniasis
**Bacterial Vaginosis (BV)** — MOST common cause of abnormal discharge
• **Cause**: Replacement of lactobacilli by Gardnerella, Atopobium, etc.
• **Amsel's Criteria** (≥3 of 4 needed):
  1. Thin, gray-white, homogeneous discharge
  2. **Whiff test positive** (fishy odor with KOH)
  3. pH >4.5
  4. **Clue cells** on wet mount (>20% of epithelial cells)
• **Treatment**: Metronidazole 500mg BID ×7 days OR metronidazole gel 0.75% ×5 days
• **Nugent Score**: Gold standard Gram stain (0-10; ≥7 = BV)

**Vulvovaginal Candidiasis (VVC)**
• **Cause**: Candida albicans (90%), C. glabrata (resistant!)
• **Symptoms**: Thick, white, 'cottage cheese' discharge + pruritus + erythema
• **Diagnosis**: KOH wet mount → pseudohyphae visible
• **Treatment**: 
  - Uncomplicated: Fluconazole 150mg PO ×1 or OTC azole ×1-7 days
  - Complicated (recurrent, severe, non-albicans): Fluconazole 150mg q72h ×3 doses + maintenance ×6 months

**Trichomoniasis** — STI, MUST treat partner!
• **Cause**: Trichomonas vaginalis (flagellated protozoan)
• **Symptoms**: Frothy, yellow-green, malodorous discharge, strawberry cervix, dysuria
• **Diagnosis**: Wet mount → motile trichomonads (low sensitivity 60-70%); NAAT is gold standard
• **Treatment**: Metronidazole 2g PO ×1 OR Tinidazole 2g PO ×1
• **Key**: Treat partner! Screen for HIV and other STIs.

[Visual: Three-column comparison table: Discharge appearance, pH, KOH whiff test, microscopy findings, treatment for each condition]

**Memory Trick:** **VAGINAL**: **V**aginitis = **A**msel's (BV), **G**reen frothy (Trich), **I**tchy cottage cheese (Yeast), **N**-AAT for Trich, **A**zoles for yeast, **L**actobacillus repletion

🔥 **Don't treat BV or Trich with just the patient — partners matter!** Trich requires partner treatment (metronidazole 2g ×1) or reinfection is guaranteed. BV: no clear benefit from routine partner treatment, but recommend condoms during treatment. **Also**: Fluconazole is CONTRAINDICATED in pregnancy (high-dose linked to miscarriage). Use vaginal azoles instead for pregnant patients.

### Abnormal Pap Smear: ASCCP 2019 Guidelines
**Cervical Cancer Screening Schedule** (USPSTF/ACOG):
• Age 21-29: Pap alone q3 years
• Age 30-65: Pap + HPV co-testing q5 years (preferred) OR Pap alone q3 years
• Age >65: Discontinue if adequate negative prior screening

**Interpreting Results**:

| Result | Next Step |
|---|---|
| **NILM** (negative) | Routine screening |
| **ASC-US** + HPV neg | Routine screening (co-test) or repeat Pap in 1yr (cytology alone) |
| **ASC-US** + HPV pos | **Colposcopy** |
| **LSIL** | Colposcopy (or reflex HPV triage if cotesting shows HPV+) |
| **ASC-H** | Colposcopy + endocervical sampling |
| **HSIL** | Colposcopy + endocervical sampling → risk of CIN2+ is high |
| **AGC** (atypical glandular) | Colposcopy + endocervical sampling + endometrial biopsy (if >35 or bleeding) |

**Management of CIN**:
• **CIN 1** (mild dysplasia): Observation if ASC-US/LSIL and colposcopy adequate (80% regress in 2 years)
• **CIN 2/3**: Excision (LEEP, cold knife cone) — do not observe unless pregnant or special circumstances
• **HPV 16/18**: Higher risk of progression — more aggressive follow-up

**HPV Vaccine**: Gardasil-9 covers 9 high-risk types. Recommended ages 9-26 (catch-up 27-45 with shared decision-making). Prevents 90% of cervical cancers!

[Visual: Algorithm flowchart from Pap result → ASC-US/LSIL/ASC-H/HSIL/AGC → Colposcopy vs follow-up timing → Management by pathology result]

**Q:** A 32-year-old has frothy yellow-green discharge, pH 6.0, and a strawberry cervix. What is the most likely diagnosis?
  [ ] Bacterial vaginosis
  [ ] Candidiasis
  [✓] Trichomoniasis
  [ ] Cervicitis from gonorrhea
  *Frothy yellow-green discharge, elevated pH, and strawberry cervix (petechiae) are classic for Trichomonas vaginalis. Confirm with NAAT and treat with metronidazole 2g PO ×1.*

**Q:** A 35-year-old patient has ASC-US on Pap with positive high-risk HPV. Next step?
  [ ] Repeat Pap in 1 year
  [ ] HPV vaccine now
  [✓] Colposcopy
  [ ] LEEP procedure
  *Per ASCCP 2019 guidelines: ASC-US + HPV positive → colposcopy. LEEP is treatment for CIN2+, not the initial diagnostic step.*

## 💊 Polypharmacy: The American Geriatrics Society Beer's Criteria

### Why This Matters
Harold, 82, comes in with his daughter after a fall. She brings a brown paper bag with 14 prescription bottles. 'He's seeing a cardiologist, a urologist, a rheumatologist, and his PCP. I don't think anyone knows everything he's on.' Harold is on amitriptyline (for 'nerve pain'), diazepam (for 'muscle spasms'), diphenhydramine (OTC for sleep), and glipizide (which caused a hypoglycemic last week). Four potentially inappropriate medications — and the fall was predictable. Polypharmacy is the #1 modifiable risk factor for adverse events in older adults.

[Visual: Drawing of a paper bag overflowing with pill bottles, with warning signs around certain medications]

### Polypharmacy & Beer's Criteria 2023
**Polypharmacy** = ≥5 medications (often associated with higher risk of adverse outcomes)
**Hyperpolypharmacy** = ≥10 medications

**Beer's Criteria 2023 (AGS)**: Potentially Inappropriate Medications (PIMs) to avoid or use with caution in adults ≥65.

**HIGH-RISK PIMs to AVOID** — memorize these for boards:

| Drug | Why Avoid | Better Alternative |
|---|---|---|
| **Anticholinergics** (diphenhydramine, hydroxyzine, oxybutynin, amitriptyline) | Confusion, falls, constipation, urinary retention | Non-sedating antihistamines, gabapentin, topical oxybutynin |
| **Benzodiazepines** (diazepam, lorazepam, alprazolam) | Falls, cognitive impairment, tolerance, withdrawal | SSRI/SNRI for anxiety, melatonin for sleep |
| **Z-drugs** (zolpidem, zaleplon) | Same fall risk as benzos! | CBT-I, melatonin, trazodone low dose |
| **NSAIDs** (chronic use) | GI bleed, AKI, HTN, HF exacerbation | Acetaminophen, topical analgesics |
| **Sulfonylureas** (glyburide/glibenclamide) | Prolonged hypoglycemia (especially glyburide!) | Metformin, DPP-4 inhibitors, low-dose glipizide |
| **Skeletal muscle relaxants** (cyclobenzaprine, carisoprodol) | Anticholinergic, sedation, falls | PT, massage, acetaminophen |
| **PPIs** beyond 8 weeks (without indication) | C. diff, osteoporosis, B12 deficiency, CKD | H2 blockers, deprescribe when appropriate |

**Anticholinergic Burden**: Higher total anticholinergic load = more cognitive impairment, falls, mortality.

[Visual: Table format with three columns: Medication, Why Avoid (with icon), Alternative (green checkmark)]

**Memory Trick:** **BAD FALL**: **B**enzos, **A**nticholinergics, **D**iphenhydramine, **F**lexeril (cyclobenzaprine), **A**lcohol (interactions), **L**ong-acting sulfonylureas, **L**ong-term PPIs

🔥 **Deprescribing is a skill**: Don't stop everything at once. Taper benzodiazepines (don't cold-turkey — withdrawal seizures!), stop one drug at a time and monitor. Use the **STOPP/START criteria** alongside Beer's for a comprehensive medication review. And remember: **'Start low, go slow, but GO'** — under-treating is also harmful.

### Medication Reconciliation: The Brown Bag Review
**Do this at EVERY visit for patients ≥65 on ≥5 meds**:

✅ Ask patient to bring ALL medications (Rx, OTC, supplements, herbals) in a bag
✅ Review each medication for: indication, dose, duration, adherence
✅ Apply **Beer's Criteria** — flag PIMs
✅ Apply **STOPP Criteria** (Screening Tool of Older Persons' Prescriptions)
✅ Apply **START Criteria** (Screening Tool to Alert to Right Treatment) — are they missing indicated meds? (e.g., osteoporosis Tx, statin)
✅ Check for **drug-drug interactions** (use online checker or consult pharmacist)
✅ Check **renal function** — adjust doses for CrCl <30
✅ **One new medication?** Consider stopping an old one (the 'one in, one out' rule)
✅ **Document rationale** for each continued potentially inappropriate medication (shared decision-making)
✅ Set a **deprescribing plan** with timeline and follow-up

[Visual: Stepwise flowchart showing the brown bag review process with decision points for each medication: Indicated? Effective? Safe? Can we stop/reduce?]

**Q:** An 80-year-old man is on amitriptyline 50mg for neuropathy. According to Beer's Criteria, what should you do?
  [ ] Increase dose for better pain control
  [ ] Continue - it's approved for neuropathy
  [✓] Taper and switch to gabapentin or duloxetine
  [ ] Add diphenhydramine for sleep
  *Amitriptyline is a high-risk anticholinergic per Beer's Criteria. Alternatives for neuropathy include gabapentin, pregabalin, or duloxetine — less anticholinergic burden.*

**Q:** Which statement about deprescribing in older adults is TRUE?
  [ ] Stop all potentially inappropriate medications at once
  [✓] Taper benzodiazepines gradually to prevent withdrawal
  [ ] Never stop medications started by a specialist
  [ ] Supplements don't count in medication review
  *Benzodiazepines require gradual tapering to prevent withdrawal seizures/rebound anxiety. Stopping all meds at once is dangerous. Always coordinate with specialists when deprescribing.*

## 🦶 Falls Prevention: Screening, Assessment, Intervention

### Why This Matters
Edna, 78, 'just tripped over the rug' and fractured her hip. She went from independent living to a skilled nursing facility. Six months later, she's still there — deconditioned, depressed, and at high risk for another fall. Falls are the LEADING cause of fatal and non-fatal injuries in adults ≥65. 1 in 4 older adults falls each year. Of those who fall, 50% can't get up without help — leading to 'long lies' (hypothermia, dehydration, rhabdomyolysis). Every well-check includes fall screening for a reason.

[Visual: Infographic showing the cascade: Fall → Hip fracture → Surgery → Immobilization → Deconditioning → Loss of independence → Nursing home]

### Fall Risk Assessment: STEADI Algorithm
**CDC STEADI** (Stopping Elderly Accidents, Deaths & Injuries):

**Screen ALL adults ≥65 annually**: "Have you fallen in the past year?"

**If YES**:
→ Assess gait/balance with **Timed Up and Go (TUG)** test
→ Multifactorial risk assessment
→ Individualized intervention

**If NO** but unsteady gait or fear of falling:
→ TUG test + focused prevention

**Timed Up and Go (TUG) Test**:
• Patient stands from chair, walks 10 ft, turns, walks back, sits down
• **Normal**: <12 seconds
• **Impaired**: ≥12 seconds (high fall risk!)
• **Cutoff for frailty**: ≥13.5 seconds

**Other Screening Tools**:
• **Morse Fall Scale**: Inpatient assessment (score ≥45 = high risk)
• **Berg Balance Scale**: 14-item balance assessment (higher score = better)
• **4-Stage Balance Test**: Can they stand with feet side-by-side, semi-tandem, tandem, and single-leg?

[Visual: Diagram showing TUG test path: Chair → 10 ft walk → Turn → Walk back → Sit. Timer icon showing <12s = normal, ≥12s = fall risk]

**Memory Trick:** **TUG Test**: **T**ime **U**p and **G**o — 12 seconds or more = **F**all risk, **L**ost balance, **A**ssistance needed, **L**ow mobility

### Multifactorial Fall Prevention
**Falls are MULTIFACTORIAL** — address ALL contributing factors:

| Domain | Assess For | Intervention |
|---|---|---|
| **Medications** | Beer's PIMs (benzos, anticholinergics, sedatives, antihypertensives) | Deprescribe, reduce, or substitute |
| **Vision** | Cataracts, glaucoma, macular degeneration, bifocal issues | Ophthalmology referral, update glasses, avoid bifocals when walking |
| **Home hazards** | Throw rugs, poor lighting, cords, clutter, grab bars, railings | Home safety evaluation (OT referral), remove rugs, nightlights |
| **Orthostatic BP** | Check lying + standing BP (drop >20 systolic = abnormal) | Hydrate, reduce antihypertensives, compression stockings |
| **Gait/balance** | TUG test, 4-stage balance test | PT/OT referral, strength training, Tai Chi, walking program |
| **Footwear** | Ill-fitting shoes, barefoot, slippers | Proper footwear with nonslip soles |
| **Bone health** | Osteoporosis screening (DXA) | Calcium, vitamin D, bisphosphonates if indicated |
| **Vitamin D** | Deficiency common | ≥800 IU/day vitamin D reduces fall risk (USPSTF: clear benefit only with vitamin D + calcium) |

**Hip Fracture Prevention**: Osteoporosis treatment + fall prevention = 50% reduction.

**After a fall**: Assess for injury (X-ray if focal pain, CT head if anticoagulated + head strike), fear of falling (activity restriction causes MORE deconditioning → MORE falls), and consider alert system (Life Alert).

[Visual: Wheel diagram with 8 spokes: each spoke is a risk domain with assessment tool and intervention]

🔥 **Orthostatic hypotension is underdiagnosed**: Check orthostatics in EVERY older patient — especially if on antihypertensives, diuretics, or alpha-blockers (tamsulosin!). BP drop >20/10 or symptoms upon standing = abnormal. Don't just check it once; symptoms are often post-prandial (after meals) or in the morning. Also: **Vitamin D 800 IU/day + calcium reduces falls by ~20%** — cheap, safe, effective.

**Q:** A 75-year-old woman completes the TUG test in 15 seconds. What does this indicate?
  [ ] Normal gait and balance
  [ ] Mild impairment, no intervention needed
  [✓] High fall risk — need multifactorial assessment
  [ ] Results are invalid, repeat test
  *TUG ≥12 seconds = impaired mobility/high fall risk. 15 seconds warrants full multifactorial fall risk assessment and individualized intervention plan.*

**Q:** Which of the following is the STRONGEST modifiable risk factor for falls in older adults?
  [ ] Cataracts requiring surgery
  [✓] Use of benzodiazepines or anticholinergics
  [ ] Lack of handrails in bathroom
  [ ] Poorly fitting shoes
  *Medications — particularly benzodiazepines, anticholinergics, and sedatives — are among the strongest modifiable risk factors for falls. Deprescribing these has a major impact.*

## 🧠 Cognitive Impairment & Frailty: Beyond 'Getting Old'

### Why This Matters
Robert, 74, was a retired accountant who 'was always sharp.' His wife noticed he was repeating stories, missing bill payments, and getting lost driving to the grocery store. 'I thought it was just old age,' she said during the visit. It wasn't — it was Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) that progressed to Alzheimer's dementia over 3 years. Differentiating normal aging from MCI from dementia — and frailty from simple deconditioning — determines whether you can intervene early, when treatments matter most.

[Visual: Three-panel comparison: Normal aging (mild forgetfulness, no functional decline), MCI (noticeable memory changes, preserved function), Dementia (memory + functional decline)]

### Normal Aging vs. MCI vs. Dementia
**Normal Cognitive Aging**:
• Slower processing speed, occasional word-finding difficulty
• **Intact**: Daily function, independence, ability to learn new information
• Memory: Forgets details but recalls later, remembers recent conversations

**Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI)** — 'The Gray Zone':
• **Objective cognitive decline** (reported by patient or informant)
• **Preserved functional independence** (can manage meds, finances, driving — just slower/harder)
• Does NOT meet criteria for dementia
• **Subtypes**: Amnestic (memory-predominant) vs. non-amnestic (executive function, language, visuospatial)
• **Progression**: 10-15% per year convert to dementia

**Dementia**:
• Cognitive decline in ≥2 domains (memory, language, executive function, visuospatial)
• **Functional impairment** — CANNOT manage daily activities independently
• Multiple types: Alzheimer's (60-80%), Vascular, Lewy Body, Frontotemporal

**Screening Tools**:
• **Mini-Cog** (3-item recall + clock draw) — 3 minutes, sensitivity 76-99%
• **MoCA** (Montreal Cognitive Assessment) — 10 minutes, more sensitive for MCI
• **SLUMS** (Saint Louis University Mental Status) — good alternative to MMSE
• **MMSE** — widely known but less sensitive for MCI, copyright issues

**When to screen**: Medicare Annual Wellness Visit requires cognitive screening if concerns reported or observed.

[Visual: Venn diagram showing overlap and differences between normal aging, MCI, and dementia with functional independence as key differentiator]

**Memory Trick:** **DEMENTIA vs MCI**: Does the patient still **M**anage **C**ash and **I**ndependent living? If **NO** → Dementia. If **YES** → consider MCI.

### Frailty: The FRAIL Scale & Sarcopenia
**Frailty** ≠ disability or comorbidity. It's a biologic syndrome of decreased physiologic reserve and vulnerability to stressors.

**FRAIL Scale** (5 questions, each 1 point):
1. **F**atigue: 'How much of the time did you feel tired in the past month?' (most/all of the time)
2. **R**esistance: Difficulty walking up 10 steps without resting
3. **A**mbulation: Difficulty walking several blocks alone
4. **I**llness: >5 comorbidities
5. **L**oss of weight: >5% body weight in past year

**Score**: 0 = Robust, 1-2 = Pre-frail, ≥3 = Frail

**Sarcopenia** = Age-related loss of muscle mass + strength + function
• **Diagnosis**: Low muscle mass (DXA, BIA, calf circumference <31cm), low handgrip strength (<16kg women, <27kg men), slow gait speed (<0.8 m/s)
• **Consequences**: Falls, fractures, disability, mortality

**Management**:
• **Resistance training** — STRONGEST intervention for sarcopenia (2-3×/week)
• **Protein intake** ≥1.2 g/kg/day (versus 0.8 for younger adults)
• **Vitamin D** ≥800 IU/day
• **Treat underlying causes**: Hypogonadism (testosterone if indicated), thyroid disease, vitamin B12 deficiency
• **Avoid**: Routine use of appetite stimulants (limited evidence, more side effects)

[Visual: Visual of the FRAIL scale as a 5-rung ladder with icons for each letter, showing progression from robust to frail]

🔥 **Delirium is a MEDICAL EMERGENCY** — it is NOT dementia! Acute onset, fluctuating consciousness, inattention = delirium until proven otherwise. Common causes: **DELIRIUM** mnemonic = **D**rugs, **E**lectrolyte, **L**ow oxygen, **I**nfection, **R**etention (urinary/fecal), **I**mpaction, **U**ncontrolled pain, **M**etabolic. And **don't miss thyroid** — myxedema coma can present as dementia in older adults.

### Differential for Cognitive Decline: The 'DEMENTIA' Workup
**Rule OUT reversible causes before diagnosing dementia**:

✅ **Vitamin B12** — deficiency causes reversible cognitive decline
✅ **TSH** — hypothyroidism mimics dementia
✅ **BMP** — electrolyte abnormalities, hypercalcemia, hyponatremia
✅ **Lyme** (if endemic) — neuroborreliosis
✅ **RPR or syphilis screen** — neurosyphilis (rare but reversible)
✅ **CT/MRI head** — rule out: normal pressure hydrocephalus, subdural hematoma, tumor, stroke, extensive white matter disease
✅ **HIV screen** (if risk factors) — HIV-associated neurocognitive disorder (HAND)
✅ **Medication review** — anticholinergics, benzodiazepines, opioids are COMMON causes of pseudo-dementia
✅ **Depression screening** — pseudodementia of depression (treat depression and cognition improves!)
✅ **Sleep apnea screen** — STOP-BANG questionnaire

[Visual: Organized checklist with lab tube icons for blood tests and brain icon for imaging, each with checkmark space]

**Q:** An 80-year-old man can still manage his finances and medications independently, but his wife reports he repeats stories. What is the MOST likely classification?
  [ ] Normal aging
  [✓] Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI)
  [ ] Dementia
  [ ] Delirium
  *MCI = objective cognitive decline with preserved functional independence. Dementia requires functional impairment (can't manage daily activities). Delirium has acute onset with fluctuating consciousness.*

**Q:** According to the FRAIL scale, a patient with a score of 3 out of 5 is classified as:
  [ ] Robust
  [ ] Pre-frail
  [✓] Frail
  [ ] Disabled
  *FRAIL scale: 0 = Robust, 1-2 = Pre-frail, ≥3 = Frail. Frail patients are at highest risk for falls, hospitalization, and mortality.*

## 📅 Prenatal Visit Schedule: What and When

### Why This Matters
Danielle, 28, G1P0, missed her first prenatal visit until 18 weeks because 'I didn't know I was supposed to go that early.' She missed first-trimester screening, the NT ultrasound window closed, and she ended up needing CVS instead of NIPT for reassurance. Then at 28 weeks, she came in with a headache and blurry vision — PIH was missed because her BP wasn't checked regularly. The prenatal visit schedule isn't arbitrary; every visit has a purpose, and missing windows has real consequences. As an FNP, you'll be the one managing most prenatal care until delivery.

[Visual: Pregnancy timeline from conception to 40 weeks with markers showing when each screening/test occurs]

### Prenatal Visit Schedule (ACOG Guidelines)
**Standard Schedule for Uncomplicated Pregnancy**:

| Gestation | Visit Frequency | Key Deliverables |
|---|---|---|
| **<28 wk** | Every 4 weeks | Establish dating, baseline labs, nuchal translucency, anatomy scan |
| **28-36 wk** | Every 2 weeks | GDM screening, Rhogam (28 wk), Tdap (27-36 wk), growth scan |
| **36 wk to delivery** | Weekly | Group B Strep screen, cervical checks (optional), presentation, fetal movement counts |

**First Visit (8-12 wk)** — The BIG one:
✅ Confirm pregnancy (urine/blood hCG, viability US)
✅ **Dating ultrasound** (most accurate 8-12 wk — CRL)
✅ Complete history: LMP, prior pregnancies, medical/surgical, meds, family/genetic
✅ **Standard labs**: CBC, blood type + Rh, antibody screen, rubella IgG, VDRL/RPR, HBsAg, HIV, varicella IgG, pap, urine culture
✅ NIPT (cfDNA) for aneuploidy screening — offered to ALL, recommended age ≥35
✅ Nuchal translucency US (11-13.6 wk)
✅ Counseling: Nutrition (prenatal vitamins!), exercise, medication safety, red flags

**18-22 Week Visit**: **Anatomy scan** — fetal survey, placental location, cervical length

[Visual: Calendar-style graphic showing pregnancy divided into trimesters with visit frequency icons and key screening milestones]

**Memory Trick:** **FIRST AID for first visit**: **F**undal height, **I**mmunizations/rubella, **R**h type, **S**TIs (HIV, RPR, hep B), **T**SH, **A**neuploidy screening, **I**ron stores (CBC), **D**ating ultrasound

### Screening Tests — When & Why
**First Trimester**:
• **NIPT (cfDNA)**: Screens for trisomies 21, 18, 13 — sensitivity >99% for T21. Can detect sex chromosome abnormalities.
• **NT Ultrasound**: Nuchal translucency ≥3.5mm → higher risk of aneuploidy + structural heart defects
• **Carrier screening**: Cystic fibrosis, spinal muscular atrophy, hemoglobinopathies — ideally prior to or early in pregnancy

**Second Trimester**:
• **Maternal serum AFP (quad screen)**: Open neural tube defects (ONTDs), ventral wall defects, multiple gestation. Done 16-18 wk (making way to cfDNA but still used when NIPT unavailable)
• **Anatomy scan (18-22 wk)**: Detailed fetal anatomy survey; if cervical length <25mm → short cervix, Tocolysis + progesterone

**24-28 Weeks**:
• **1-hour Glucose Challenge Test (GCT)**: 50g glucose, check at 1 hour → ≥140 mg/dL = abnormal → proceed to 3-hour GTT
• **Oral Glucose Tolerance Test (OGTT)**: 100g glucose, fasting + 1, 2, 3 hour levels
  - Carpenter-Coustan criteria: Fasting ≥95, 1h ≥180, 2h ≥155, 3h ≥140 (any 2 abnormal = GDM)

**36 Weeks**:
• **Group B Streptococcus (GBS)**: Vaginal-rectal swab → if positive, intrapartum penicillin

**If Rh negative**: **Rhogam (RhoGAM)** at 28 wk AND within 72 hours of any bleeding, trauma, amniocentesis, or delivery

[Visual: Timeline infographic with gestational weeks on x-axis and screening tests shown as icons placed at their appropriate timing windows]

🔥 **Don't miss Tdap in EVERY pregnancy!** Tdap (27-36 wk) provides passive immunity to newborn against pertussis. Does NOT matter when last Tdap was — give in EVERY pregnancy regardless of prior vaccination interval. Also: **COVID-19 and flu vaccines are SAFE in any trimester**. Recommend them.

**Q:** A G1P0 patient at 12 weeks comes for her first visit. Which of the following is NOT typically done at this visit?
  [ ] Dating ultrasound
  [ ] NIPT (cfDNA) screening
  [✓] Group B Strep culture
  [ ] Standard prenatal labs (CBC, type/Rh, HIV)
  *GBS screening is done at 36 weeks. First visit includes dating US, NIPT offer, and standard prenatal labs.*

**Q:** When should Tdap vaccine be given during pregnancy?
  [ ] First trimester only
  [✓] 27-36 weeks gestation
  [ ] Anytime during pregnancy
  [ ] Only if the patient hasn't had Tdap in 10 years

## 🤢 Common Pregnancy Complaints: What's Normal, What's Not

### Why This Matters
Tara, 26, G3P2, is 8 weeks pregnant and 'can't keep anything down.' She's lost 8 pounds in 2 weeks, has ketones in her urine, and her husband is terrified. She's been told 'morning sickness is normal' but she's vomited 6 times today. This is hyperemesis gravidarum — not 'normal' morning sickness — and she needs treatment. On the other end of the spectrum, Emily, 34 weeks, is worried about swelling in her ankles and hands. Is it normal edema or preeclampsia? Differentiating routine discomfort from dangerous complications is the skill that separates a good FNP from a great one.

[Visual: Split illustration: Left side shows mild symptoms (normal pregnancy), right side shows red-flag symptoms requiring urgent evaluation]

### Nausea & Vomiting of Pregnancy vs. Hyperemesis Gravidarum
**Nausea & Vomiting of Pregnancy (NVP)** — 'Morning sickness':
• 70-80% of pregnancies, peaks at 9 weeks, resolves by 16-20 weeks
• Mild to moderate, doesn't cause significant dehydration or weight loss
• **Treatment**: Dietary changes (small frequent meals, bland foods, ginger 250mg QID, vitamin B6 10-25mg TID)

**Hyperemesis Gravidarum (HG)** — Severe form:
• **Persistent vomiting** causing:
  - >5% weight loss from pre-pregnancy weight
  - Dehydration + ketonuria
  - Electrolyte abnormalities (hypokalemia, metabolic alkalosis)
  - Nutritional deficiencies (thiamine!) → risk of Wernicke encephalopathy
• **Treatment**:
  1. **First-line**: Doxylamine 12.5mg + Pyridoxine 25mg TID (Diclegis/Bonjesta — FDA-approved!)
  2. **Second-line**: Ondansetron (Zofran) — caution: debate about cleft palate risk, benefit usually outweighs risk
  3. **Third-line**: Metoclopramide, promethazine, or prochlorperazine
  4. **Refractory**: IV fluids + electrolyte repletion, TPN if severe, steroids (last resort)
  - Always give **thiamine (B1)** before IV dextrose to prevent Wernicke's!

**Warning Signs requiring evaluation**: Weight loss >5%, ketonuria, inability to keep fluids down 24+ hours, abdominal pain, fever

[Visual: Comparison table contrasting NVP (mild, self-limited) with HG (severe, weight loss, dehydration, needs treatment)]

### Other Common Complaints & Management
**GERD / Heartburn** (30-50% of pregnancies):
• **Mechanism**: Progesterone relaxes LES + enlarging uterus increases intra-abdominal pressure
• **Treatment**: Antacids (calcium carbonate, Tums), H2 blockers (ranitidine/famotidine — safe!), PPIs (omeprazole — safe!)
• **Lifestyle**: Small meals, avoid lying flat after eating, elevate head of bed

**Low Back Pain** (50-80%):
• **Mechanism**: Relaxin + lordosis from growing belly
• **Treatment**: PT, acetaminophen (AVOID NSAIDs/ibuprofen!), maternity support belt, warm compresses
• **Red flag**: Back pain + fever + dysuria = pyelonephritis! Get urinalysis

**Edema** (legs, feet, hands):
• **Normal**: Dependent edema, worse at day's end, improves with elevation, no other symptoms
• **ABNORMAL (worrisome)**: Sudden onset, facial/hand edema, +HTN, +proteinuria → **pre-eclampsia!**
• **Management**: Elevation, compression stockings, avoid prolonged standing, hydration

**Round Ligament Pain** (sharp groin pain with movement):
• **Reassurance** — normal stretching! Sudden movement triggers brief sharp pain
• Red flag if pain is constant, fever, vomiting, or accompanied by vaginal bleeding

**Shortness of Breath**:
• **Normal**: Progesterone increases respiratory drive, enlarged uterus → decreased lung volume
• **ABNORMAL**: Sudden onset, pleuritic, with chest pain, or with unilateral leg swelling → PE risk (pregnancy is hypercoagulable!)

[Visual: Body diagram showing a pregnant silhouette with numbered areas: 1. Head (HA → preeclampsia), 2. Chest (SOB → PE?), 3. Upper abdomen (GERD), 4. Lower back, 5. Legs (edema, DVT)]

🔥 **Medication safety in pregnancy simplified**: Tylenol (acetaminophen) = safe. Ibuprofen/NSAIDs = AVOID (risk of oligohydramnios, premature ductus arteriosus closure — especially after 20 wk). Most antibiotics = safe (penicillins, cephalosporins, macrolides except erythromycin estolate). DOXYCYCLINE and TETRACYCLINE = contraindicated (bone/tooth effects). **Levothyroxine doses often need to INCREASE by 30-50% in pregnancy** — check TSH every 4 weeks!

**Q:** A patient at 10 weeks has lost 7% of her pre-pregnancy weight, has ketones in urine, and vomits 5+ times daily. What is the MOST appropriate diagnosis?
  [ ] Normal morning sickness — reassure
  [✓] Hyperemesis gravidarum — start doxylamine + pyridoxine
  [ ] Gastroenteritis — prescribe antibiotics
  [ ] Preeclampsia — admit for monitoring
  *Weight loss >5%, ketonuria, and persistent vomiting despite conservative measures = hyperemesis gravidarum. First-line treatment is doxylamine + pyridoxine (Diclegis).*

**Q:** Which of the following symptoms in a 34-week pregnant patient requires immediate evaluation?
  [ ] Mild ankle swelling at the end of the day
  [ ] Heartburn after eating spicy food
  [ ] Sharp groin pain with sudden movement
  [✓] New onset facial edema with headache and visual changes
  *Facial edema + headache + visual changes = preeclampsia until proven otherwise. Check BP and urine protein. The other options are common, benign pregnancy complaints.*

## ⚠️ High-Risk Conditions: When to Refer

### Why This Matters
Keisha, 32, G2P1, has a history of C-section. She's now 39 weeks and feels a 'tearing' sensation in her lower abdomen. She had an appointment tomorrow but didn't think this was urgent. Her husband insisted she come in. Her vitals are stable, but her abdomen is tender over the previous C-section scar, and FHR tracing shows variable decelerations. Uterine rupture is a life-threatening emergency. Recognizing high-risk conditions and knowing when to refer — or when to send straight to the ER — is critical for safe FNP practice.

[Visual: Warning sign icons: pregnant silhouette with red exclamation points at critical areas (abdomen, head, chest, legs)]

### Hypertensive Disorders of Pregnancy
**Classification** (ACOG):

| Condition | BP | Proteinuria | Other Features |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Chronic HTN** | ≥140/90 before 20 wk | ± | Preexisting |
| **Gestational HTN** | ≥140/90 after 20 wk | Negative | No systemic features |
| **Preeclampsia** | ≥140/90 after 20 wk | ≥300mg/24h or ≥1+ dipstick | May have end-organ dysfunction |
| **Eclampsia** | ≥140/90 | ± | +Seizures |
| **HELLP** | ± | ± | Hemolysis, Elevated Liver enzymes, Low Platelets |

**Preeclampsia Warning Signs (CRUSH)**:
• **C**loudy vision / **C**onfusion
• **R**ight upper quadrant pain (liver capsule distension)
• **U**rine output decreased
• **S**evere headache
• **H**ypertension + edema

**Crescendo pattern**: Gestational HTN → mild preeclampsia → severe preeclampsia → eclampsia → HELLP

**Prevention**: Low-dose aspirin 81mg daily starting at 12-16 wk for HIGH-RISK patients (prior preeclampsia, multifetal gestation, CKD, DM, chronic HTN, autoimmune disease)

**Treatment**: Delivery is the only cure! Mild: manage outpatient with BP control. Severe: admit, give magnesium for seizure prophylaxis, deliver.

**Postpartum**: Risk persists for 6-12 weeks postpartum. BP can worsen at 3-6 days postpartum (fluid mobilization)!

[Visual: Staircase diagram showing progression from chronic/gestational HTN → preeclampsia → severe preeclampsia → eclampsia → HELLP with defining features at each step]

**Memory Trick:** **CRUSH** for preeclampsia warning signs: **C**loudy vision, **R**UQ pain, **U**rine output down, **S**evere HA, **H**TN

### Red Flags That Need Immediate Referral/ER
**OB Emergencies — Don't Miss These**:

🚨 **Preeclampsia with Severe Features**:
• BP ≥160/110 (confirmed in 15 min)
• Severe headache, visual changes, RUQ pain
• Pulmonary edema
• Platelets <100K, Cr >1.1, AST/ALT >2× normal
→ **Call OB!** Likely need admission, magnesium, delivery

🚨 **Eclampsia**: Seizure + HTN/proteinuria → ABCs, magnesium 4-6g IV, emergency C-section

🚨 **Vaginal Bleeding in 3rd Trimester**:
• **Placenta previa** (painless bright red bleeding) — NO vaginal exam! STAT ultrasound
• **Placental abruption** (painful dark bleeding + rigid/firm uterus) — fetal distress → C-section

🚨 **PPROM (Preterm Premature Rupture of Membranes)**:
• Gush of fluid + pooling on speculum exam + positive ferning/nitrazine
• <37 weeks → steroids for fetal lung maturity, antibiotics (group B strep coverage), monitor for chorioamnionitis

🚨 **Decreased Fetal Movement**:
• <10 movements in 2 hours in 3rd trimester
• Kick counts: Should feel ≥10 kicks in 2 hours after a meal
• → NST (non-stress test) + BPP (biophysical profile)

🚨 **VTE / DVT / PE in Pregnancy**:
• Unilateral leg swelling/pain, chest pain, SOB
• Pregnancy is 5× higher VTE risk (hypercoagulable state!)
• Workup: venous Doppler, CT pulmonary angiogram or V/Q scan
• Treatment: LMWH (enoxaparin) — NOT warfarin (teratogenic)

🚨 **Postpartum Red Flags**:
• Hemorrhage (>500mL vag delivery, >1000mL C-section)
• Endometritis (fever + fundal tenderness + foul lochia)
• Postpartum preeclampsia (can occur up to 6 weeks after delivery!)

[Visual: ER-style triage board with each emergency listed as a case with symptoms, vitals, and action required]

🔥 **Crash C-section readiness**: When you send a patient with suspected uterine rupture, placental abruption with fetal distress, or cord prolapse to the ER — call ahead and say: 'I'm sending a potentially crashing OB patient.' STAT page the OB team. Minutes matter. Also: **Magnesium is the drug for eclampsia prophylaxis**, NOT phenytoin or diazepam. Know the magnesium dosing: 4-6g IV loading over 15-20 min, then 2g/hr maintenance.

**Q:** A G3P2 at 32 weeks presents with a painless gush of fluid and positive ferning on exam. What is the NEXT step?
  [ ] Schedule induction now
  [✓] Admit for observation, give betamethasone × 2 doses
  [ ] Reassure and send home with follow-up
  [ ] Check NST and discharge if reassuring
  *PPROM <37 weeks → admit, give corticosteroids (betamethasone) for fetal lung maturity, monitor for chorioamnionitis, and plan delivery timing. Term PROM (≥37 wks) → induce.*

**Q:** Which of the following is a severe feature of preeclampsia requiring immediate delivery?
  [ ] BP 145/95 with 1+ proteinuria
  [ ] Mild ankle edema
  [✓] BP 165/112 with headache and RUQ pain
  [ ] Gestational hypertension without proteinuria
  *BP ≥160/110 + severe headache + RUQ pain = preeclampsia with severe features. Needs immediate referral/admission for magnesium, BP control, and delivery planning.*

## 🤝 Confidential Care: Legal & Ethical Essentials

### Why This Matters
Jordan, 16, asks to 'talk to you alone' while mom is still in the room. Mom looks offended. 'We don't keep secrets from each other,' she says. Jordan's face falls. How you handle this moment determines whether Jordan ever tells you about the vaping, the unprotected sex, or the suicidal thoughts they've been having. Studies show that adolescents are LESS likely to access care if they fear their parents will be told. Confidentiality isn't optional — it's the foundation of adolescent medicine.

[Visual: Office scene: doctor asking parent to step out, teen looking relieved, balancing trust and transparency]

### Minor Consent & Confidentiality: What You CAN Keep Private
**HIPAA Privacy Rule**: Adolescents can consent for their own care in certain situations. In those cases, they control their own health information.

**Services Minors Can Consent For (varies by state, but generally)**:
• **STI testing and treatment** — ALL 50 states
• **Contraceptive services** — most states (some allow pharmacy access without parental consent)
• **Pregnancy testing and prenatal care**
• **Mental health counseling** — varies (typically 12+ sessions or specific conditions)
• **Substance use treatment** — most states
• **HIV testing** — ALL 50 states

**What MUST be disclosed**:
✅ **Abuse** (physical, sexual, emotional, neglect) — mandatory reporting in ALL states
✅ **Risk of serious harm to self or others** — duty to protect
✅ **Certain infectious diseases** — public health reporting (varies by state)

**Confidentiality Counseling Model**:
1. Start with parent present: 'I'd like to speak with Jordan alone for a few minutes, then we'll all talk together.'
2. Set ground rules with teen: 'Everything we discuss is private between us UNLESS you're going to hurt yourself, someone is hurting you, or someone is hurting you.'
3. Encourage parent-teen communication: 'It's often helpful to talk to your parents about these things. Can we think about how to do that together?'
4. Bill appropriately: Use confidential billing codes. Some states require explanation of benefits (EOBs) to be sent privately.

**Key boards point**: FNP must know STATE-specific minor consent laws. When in doubt, consult legal counsel or your state's minor consent statute.

[Visual: Flowchart: Teen enters clinic → Check-in → Parent in room → Ask parent to step out → Confidential interview → Set limits → Discuss concerns → Invite parent back for summary]

**Memory Trick:** **CONFIDENT**: **C**ontraception, **O**bstetrics (pregnancy), **N**otifiable STIs, **F**amily planning, **I**nfections (HIV), **D**rug/alcohol treatment, **E**motional (mental health), **N**eglect reporting, **T**een friendly

🔥 **EOBs can break confidentiality**: Even if you don't tell parents, an Explanation of Benefits from insurance listing 'chlamydia test' or 'contraceptive counseling' going to the policyholder (parent) can disclose sensitive services. Know your state's EOB confidentiality protections or work with Title X-funded clinics that provide free/low-cost confidential care. Also: **Don't assume** — ask the teen directly about what they want shared.

### Setting Up a Confidential Adolescent Visit
✅ **Front desk**: Ask if teen has privacy concerns about EOBs/phone calls
✅ **Vitals room**: Give confidential screening questionnaire (HEADSSS, PHQ-9, CRAFFT)
✅ **Exam room, alone**: 'Your mom/dad will join us at the end, but first I'd like to talk with you alone'
✅ **State limits clearly**: 'The only exceptions are if you're being hurt, might hurt yourself, or might hurt someone else'
✅ **Assess confidentiality understanding**: 'Do you understand what I've said?'
✅ **Complete HEADSSS assessment**
✅ **Offer parent summary**: 'What would you like me to share with your parent?'
✅ **Invite parent back**: Share agreed-upon info, provide general health guidance
✅ **Document**: Note confidentiality agreement in chart, avoid documenting sensitive details if parent has chart access

[Visual: Step-by-step visual guide through the adolescent visit workflow with confidentiality flags at each stage]

**Q:** A 15-year-old tests positive for chlamydia. Which statement is TRUE regarding confidentiality?
  [ ] Parent MUST be informed before treatment
  [✓] Minor can consent to STI treatment without parental knowledge in all 50 states
  [ ] I can only treat if parent is present
  [ ] Must report to police for statutory reasons
  *ALL 50 states allow minors to consent to STI diagnosis and treatment. The teen has the right to confidentiality, though encouraging parent communication is always best practice.*

**Q:** A 17-year-old admits to suicidal thoughts with a plan. What is your legal obligation?
  [ ] Maintain confidentiality — it was a private conversation
  [ ] Call only the police
  [✓] Breach confidentiality to protect the minor and notify parents/guardians

## ⚽ Sports Physical: The PPE Monograph

### Why This Matters
Marcus, 14, star point guard, comes in for his 'sports physical' — he needs the form signed by Friday. He's 'totally healthy.' Mom mentions he passed out during practice last week but 'it was just dehydration.' Except it wasn't — Marcus has hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM), the #1 cause of sudden cardiac death in young athletes. The sports physical (PPE) isn't just a checkbox; it's a screening that can save lives. You'll do dozens of these a month in primary care.

[Visual: Sports physical form with highlighted sections: cardiac screening questions (HCM red flags), musculoskeletal exam, concussion history]

### Pre-Participation Physical Evaluation (PPE) Monograph — 6th Edition
**Purpose**: Not to disqualify, but to IDENTIFY conditions that may increase risk and MANAGE them.

**Key Components**:
1. **Medical History** (most important part!):
   • Prior cardiac symptoms: chest pain, syncope/near-syncope, palpitations, excessive SOB with exercise
   • Family history: sudden cardiac death before age 50, HCM, long QT, Marfan syndrome
   • Prior concussion, hospitalizations, surgeries, medications

2. **Cardiac Screening** — 14-Element AHA Screening Questions:
   • Any chest pain or discomfort with exercise?
   • Ever passed out or nearly passed out during/after exercise?
   • Ever had high blood pressure or heart murmur?
   • Family member with HCM, long QT, Marfan, or sudden cardiac death <50yo?
   • Any unexplained death in family <50yo?
   **Any ‘YES’** → ECG and/or cardiology referral

3. **Physical Exam**:
   • BP, HR, auscultation (sitting, supine, standing — looking for HCM murmur that changes)
   • Visual acuity
   • MSK screening: Duck-Waddle test, strength, flexibility
   • Skin: café-au-lait spots (neurofibromatosis), acanthosis nigricans (DM risk)
   • Genital exam/hernia check: especially for contact sports (varies by state/sport)

4. **Concussion History**: Number, severity, prolonged symptoms, loss of consciousness

**When to DISQUALIFY (temporarily)**:
• Active cardiac symptoms (syncope, chest pain) → cardiology eval needed first
• Untreated hypertension (Stage 2)
• Acute illness (fever, myocarditis)
• Recent concussion (not cleared yet)
• Single functioning organ (kidney, eye, testis) → shared decision-making, protective equipment

[Visual: Sports physical form with the 14 AHA cardiac screening questions highlighted in a red box]

**Memory Trick:** **SCUBA D** for cardiac screening: **S**yncope, **C**hest pain, **U**nexplained SOB, **B**lood pressure high, **A**bnormal family history (sudden death), **D**isqualify if any YES

### Concussion Evaluation & Return-to-Play (RTP)
**Concussion Basics**:
• Traumatically induced transient disturbance of brain function
• **Symptoms**: Headache, dizziness, confusion, blurred vision, nausea, sensitivity to light/noise
• **Red flags (need ED/imaging)**: Loss of consciousness >1 min, focal neuro deficit, worsening HA, vomiting, seizure, GCS <15

**SCAT-5 (Sports Concussion Assessment Tool)**: Standardized sideline assessment
• Immediate memory, concentration, balance, coordination
• Must be done by trained professional

**Return-to-Play Protocol (5-stage, minimum 24 hrs per stage)**:
1. **Rest** — No activity, cognitive rest (no screens, school work, exercise)
2. **Light aerobic** — Walking, stationary bike (HR <70% max)
3. **Sport-specific** — Running drills, no contact
4. **Non-contact drills** — Full practice, no contact
5. **Full contact practice** — Full practice with contact
6. **Return to play** — Game clearance

**Key**: If symptoms return at ANY stage, stop and go back to previous stage. Do NOT advance until symptom-free at current stage.

**Children/Adolescents**: Take LONGER to recover than adults (up to 30 days). More conservative approach needed.

**Second Impact Syndrome**: Rare but devastating — second concussion before first resolves → diffuse cerebral swelling, 50% mortality. THIS is why RTP protocols exist!

[Visual: Ladder diagram showing 6 stages of RTP with arrows showing progression and 'symptom recurrence → go back down' pathways]

🔥 **Don't clear an athlete for sports the same day as a concussion** — pediatric guidelines say NO same-day RTP. Also: **ECG is NOT mandatory for all athletes** per PPE monograph (only if history/exam positive), but some states mandate it. Know your state law. And **BNP/NT-proBNP is NOT a cardiac screening tool in athletes** — don't order it.

**Q:** A 16-year-old athlete reports passing out during basketball practice. What should you do?
  [ ] Clear for play if ECG is normal
  [✓] Refer to cardiology before clearing for sports
  [ ] It's probably dehydration, clear with hydration instructions
  [ ] Order echocardiogram in office
  *Exercise-induced syncope is a RED FLAG for HCM, arrhythmogenic RV cardiomyopathy, or long QT. Must get cardiology eval (ECG, echo) before clearing for sports.*

**Q:** A 15-year-old soccer player with a concussion feels better 3 days later. When can she return to play?
  [ ] Tomorrow if completely symptom-free
  [ ] One week of rest then full clearance
  [✓] Wait minimum 24 hours then start staged RTP protocol, advancing only if symptom-free at each stage
  [ ] Return to play immediately if SCAT-5 is normal
  *RTP requires staged progression with minimum 24 hours per stage. No same-day return. Adolescents need more conservative management — rushing RTP risks second impact syndrome.*

## 🚨 HEADSSS: Identifying High-Risk Behaviors

### Why This Matters
Aaliyah, 17, is brought in by mom because she's 'acting moody.' Mom leaves the room. Aaliyah stares at the floor. 'Everyone asks me stuff. My grades are fine. I'm fine.' But she picks at her cuticles, avoids eye contact, and her hoodie hides significant weight loss. The HEADSSS assessment — a structured tool you'll use every single day — reveals she's been restricting calories for 8 months, vaping THC daily, and has a boyfriend who 'gets upset when I don't answer texts right away.' Three problems uncovered in 10 minutes because you asked the right questions the right way.

[Visual: HEADSSS wheel diagram with home, education, activities, drugs, sexuality, suicide/safety as spokes]

### HEADSSS Assessment: The Adolescent Psychosocial Interview
**H** — **Home**: Who lives at home? Relationship with family? Any recent moves, changes, conflicts?
**E** — **Education/Employment**: School performance, favorite subjects, any recent drop? Bullying? Job?
**A** — **Activities**: Friends, hobbies, sports, clubs? Who do you hang out with? What do you do for fun?
**D** — **Drugs**: Alcohol, tobacco, vaping, marijuana, other substances? Ask about friends’ use first (less threatening), then personal use
**S** — **Sexuality**: Attractions, relationships, sexual activity, contraception, STI screening, pregnancy?
**S** — **Suicide/Safety**: Depression, self-harm, suicidal thoughts/plans/attempts? Also: violence, safety at home/school/online
**S** — **Strengths**: What are you good at? What do you enjoy? What makes you proud? (End on positive note!)

**Timing**: Once/year for ALL adolescents starting around age 11-12, always without parent present

**CRAFFT 2.0** — Substance Use Screening (for age 12-21):
**C** — Have you ever ridden in a **C**ar driven by someone (including yourself) who was high?
**R** — Do you ever use alcohol/drugs to **R**elax?
**A** — Do you ever use alcohol/drugs **A**lone?
**F** — Do you ever **F**orget things you did while using?
**F** — Do your **F**amily/Friends ever tell you to cut down?
**T** — Have you ever gotten into **T**rouble while using?
**≥2 positive** → high risk for substance use disorder

[Visual: Mind map centered on 'HEADSSS' with branches showing specific questions for each domain]

**Memory Trick:** **HEADSSS** — **H**ome, **E**ducation, **A**ctivities, **D**rugs, **S**exuality, **S**uicide/Safety, **S**trengths. Add **+S** for **S**trengths (end positive!)

### Red Flags & When to Intervene
**Eating Disorders**:
• **Screen**: "Are you worried about your weight?" "Do you ever binge eat or purge?" (SCOFF questionnaire)
• **SCOFF** (1 point each, ≥2 = concern):
  - Do you make yourself **S**ick (vomit) because of fullness?
  - Do you worry about losing **C**ontrol over eating?
  - Have you recently lost >15 lbs (**O**ne stone) in 3 months?
  - Do you believe you're **F**at when others say you're thin?
  - Does **F**ood dominate your life?
• **Red flags**: BMI <5th percentile, bradycardia (<50 bpm), orthostatic vital changes, lanugo, electrolyte abnormalities, amenorrhea, QT prolongation on ECG

**Self-Harm / Suicidality**:
• **Ask directly**: "Have you ever thought about hurting yourself or ending your life?" (Asking does NOT increase risk!)
• **If YES**: Assess plan, means, timeline, intent. Past attempts = highest risk factor
• **Actions**: Create safety plan, remove means (guns, meds), involve trusted adult, refer to mental health, consider ER if imminent

**Vaping / E-Cigarettes**:
• **Most common substance used by teens** — 20% of high schoolers report current use
• **EVALI** (E-cigarette/Vaping-Associated Lung Injury): Respiratory symptoms + GI symptoms + fever + bilateral infiltrates on CXR
• **Management**: Screen all teens. Offer cessation resources (This is Quitting text program: 88709)

**Bullying / Cyberbullying**:
• Screen: "Has anyone at school or online ever made you feel scared, humiliated, or unsafe?"
• Associated with: Depression, suicide risk, school avoidance, somatic symptoms
• Action: Report to school, involve parents, assess for safety

**HPV Vaccine**: Strongly recommend at 11-12. Can start at age 9. Catch-up through 26.

[Visual: Triage grid with severity levels: Green (screen/reassure), Yellow (counsel/monitor), Red (immediate intervention/referral)]

🔥 **Asking about suicide does NOT plant the idea** — this is a myth. Teens feel RELIEVED when someone asks directly. Use the **Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS)** for structured assessment. Also: **Vaping is NOT 'just water vapor'** — e-liquids contain propylene glycol, glycerol, flavoring chemicals, and often heavy metals. Most 'nicotine-free' vapes still contain nicotine.

  [ ] Moderate risk, requires brief intervention
  [✓] High risk for substance use disorder, needs further assessment and intervention
  [ ] CRAFFT is invalid in this age group
  *≥2 positive on CRAFFT = high risk for substance use disorder. Needs further assessment, brief intervention, and possibly referral to treatment.*

**Q:** Which statement about adolescent suicide screening is TRUE?
  [ ] Asking about suicide increases risk of attempts

  [✓] Asking directly does NOT increase risk and may be therapeutic
  [ ] Screening is only recommended for high-risk groups

## 💔 Depression: Screening, Diagnosis, & First-Line Treatment

### Why This Matters
David, 45, successful contractor, says he's 'just tired.' He sleeps 10 hours but wakes up exhausted. He's lost interest in fishing, his marriage is strained, and he's been drinking more. He says, 'I just need a physical — something must be wrong with my thyroid.' His PHQ-9 score is 18 (severe depression). He's one of the 1 in 5 adults who will experience depression. Half of all depression is missed in primary care — where it's MOST often treated. You'll manage more depression than any other mental health condition.

[Visual: PHQ-9 chart showing severity spectrum from minimal to severe with treatment escalation at each level]

### Major Depressive Disorder: Diagnosis & Screening
**DSM-5 Criteria** (need ≥5 symptoms in SAME 2-week period, with at least one being depressed mood or anhedonia):

**SIGECAPS** mnemonic:
• **S**leep changes (insomnia or hypersomnia)
• **I**nterest loss / anhedonia
• **G**uilt / worthlessness
• **E**nergy loss / fatigue
• **C**oncentration difficulty
• **A**ppetite changes (increase or decrease)
• **P**sychomotor changes (agitation or retardation)
• **S**uicidal thoughts

**PHQ-2** (Ultra-brief screening — positive if ≥3):
"Over the past 2 weeks, how often have you been bothered by:"
1. Little interest or pleasure in doing things?
2. Feeling down, depressed, or hopeless?

**If PHQ-2 positive → administer PHQ-9**:
• **0-4**: None/minimal — no treatment needed
• **5-9**: Mild — watchful waiting, therapy, or lifestyle
• **10-14**: Moderate — medication or therapy (or both)
• **15-19**: Moderately severe — medication + therapy
• **20-27**: Severe — medication + therapy, consider psychiatry referral

**Question #9 on PHQ-9**: "Thoughts that you'd be better off dead or hurting yourself" → any score >0 requires suicide risk assessment

**Differential**: Hypothyroidism, B12 deficiency, OSA, autoimmune disease, medication side effects (beta-blockers, steroids, isotretinoin)

[Visual: PHQ-9 scoring scale visual with color bands: green (mild), yellow (moderate), orange (mod-severe), red (severe)]

**Memory Trick:** **SIGECAPS** for depression diagnosis: **S**leep, **I**nterest, **G**uilt, **E**nergy, **C**oncentration, **A**ppetite, **P**sychomotor, **S**uicidal

### Pharmacotherapy: SSRIs First-Line
**SSRIs** are FIRST-LINE for MDD. All equally efficacious — choose by side effect profile.

| Drug | Starting Dose | Target Dose | Key Points |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Escitalopram (Lexapro)** | 5-10mg | 10-20mg | Best tolerated, fewest drug interactions, best for first episode |
| **Sertraline (Zoloft)** | 25-50mg | 50-200mg | Good for comorbid anxiety, safe in pregnancy |
| **Fluoxetine (Prozac)** | 10-20mg | 20-80mg | Long half-life (good for adherence, bad if side effects), activating — take AM |
| **Paroxetine (Paxil)** | 10-20mg | 20-50mg | Most anticholinergic, weight gain, withdrawal — avoid first-line |
| **Citalopram (Celexa)** | 10-20mg | 20-40mg | Max 40mg (QT prolongation risk above 40mg) |

**Treatment Principles**:
• **Start low, go slow** — especially in elderly
• **Therapeutic response takes 4-8 weeks** — manage expectations!
• **Side effects often start before benefit** — GI upset, headache, anxiety first 1-2 weeks (usually resolve)
• **Duration**: Continue 6-12 months after remission, then consider taper
• **If no response after 8 weeks at therapeutic dose**: Switch to different SSRI or SNRI
• **If partial response after 8 weeks**: Consider augmentation (bupropion, mirtazapine, aripiprazole)

**Refer to psychiatry IF**:
• Treatment-resistant (2+ failed trials)
• Psychotic features
• Bipolar depression (or strong suspicion)
• Severe suicidal ideation
• Catatonia
• Need for ECT

[Visual: Algorithm: PHQ-9 score → Severity level → Treatment (medication type, dose, therapy referral) → Follow-up in 2-4 weeks]

🔥 **SSRI 'activation syndrome'** — especially in young adults (age <25): Fluoxetine can cause agitation, anxiety, insomnia in the first 2 weeks. Warn patients. Also: **Bupropion (Wellbutrin) is NOT for anxiety** — it's activating, can worsen anxiety, and is **contraindicated in eating disorders/seizure disorders**. And **NEVER start antidepressant monotherapy if you suspect bipolar** — can trigger mania/hypomania (screen with MDQ first!).

### Follow-Up Monitoring
✅ **2-4 weeks after starting**: Recheck PHQ-9, assess side effects, check adherence, brief suicide screen
✅ **6-8 weeks**: Reassess for response (goal: ≥50% reduction in PHQ-9)
✅ **If improving**: Continue current dose, follow monthly
✅ **If not improving**: Check adherence, increase dose if tolerated, consider switching
✅ **At remission**: Continue meds 6-12 months, discuss relapse prevention
✅ **Labs before starting**: None required unless specific concerns (TSH, B12 if fatigue prominent; ECG if citalopram or elderly)

[Visual: Calendar/follow-up timeline showing expected milestones: 2wk, 4wk, 8wk, 6mo, 12mo markers]

**Q:** A 32-year-old has a PHQ-9 score of 22 with suicidal ideation (score 2 on question 9). What is the NEXT step?
  [ ] Start SSRI and schedule follow-up in 4 weeks
  [ ] Refer to therapy only
  [✓] Assess suicide risk (plan, means, intent), discuss safety plan, involve supports, consider emergency referral
  [ ] Increase to maximum SSRI dose immediately
  *Any score >0 on PHQ-9 question 9 requires immediate suicide risk assessment. Score of 22 = severe depression. Safety first before treatment plan.*

**Q:** Which SSRI has the BEST side effect profile and is often preferred for first-episode depression?
  [ ] Fluoxetine
  [ ] Paroxetine
  [✓] Escitalopram
  [ ] Sertraline
  *Escitalopram (Lexapro) has the best tolerability, fewest drug interactions, and is well-studied. Paroxetine has the worst side effect profile.*

## 🚨 Anxiety: GAD, Panic Disorder & Treatment

### Why This Matters
Priya, 28, has been to the ER 4 times in 3 months. 'I think I'm having a heart attack,' she says each time. Her heart races, she can't breathe, she feels like she's dying. All workups — normal. She's terrified something is being missed. She's avoiding driving, exercise, and social events 'just in case.' This is panic disorder with agoraphobia. She's suffering — and costing the healthcare system thousands. Appropriate diagnosis and treatment changes everything.

[Visual: Illustration of panic attack sensations: pounding heart, chest tightness, sweating, shortness of breath, dizziness, with 'I'm dying' thought bubble]

### GAD vs. Panic Disorder vs. Social Anxiety
**Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD)**:
• Excessive, uncontrollable worry about MULTIPLE domains (work, health, family, finances) for ≥6 months
• Associated with: Restlessness, fatigue, irritability, muscle tension, sleep disturbance, poor concentration
• **GAD-7 Score**: ≥10 = moderate, ≥15 = severe
• **Differential**: Hyperthyroidism, caffeine, stimulants, OSA, medication side effects

**Panic Disorder**:
• Recurrent, UNEXPECTED panic attacks + fear of having another (or consequences of attacks)
• **Panic Attack** (sudden onset, peaks in 10 min, ≥4 of):
  - Palpitations, sweating, trembling, SOB, choking sensation, chest pain, nausea, dizziness, depersonalization, fear of losing control or dying, paresthesias, chills/hot flashes
• Often leads to **agoraphobia** — avoidance of places/situations where escape might be difficult

**Social Anxiety Disorder**:
• Marked fear of social situations where scrutiny may occur
• Fear of showing anxiety symptoms (blushing, trembling, sweating)
• Leads to avoidance of social interactions

**GAD-7 Screening**:
"Over the past 2 weeks, how often have you been bothered by:"
0 = Not at all, 1 = Several days, 2 = More than half the days, 3 = Nearly every day
1. Feeling nervous, anxious, or on edge
2. Not being able to stop or control worrying
3. Worrying too much about different things
4. Trouble relaxing
5. Being so restless it's hard to sit still
6. Becoming easily annoyed or irritable
7. Feeling afraid as if something awful might happen
Score: 0-4 minimal, 5-9 mild, 10-14 moderate, 15+ severe

[Visual: Three-column Venn diagram comparing GAD (excessive worry, >6 months), Panic Disorder (attacks + fear of attacks), Social Anxiety (fear of scrutiny)]

**Memory Trick:** **WATCHERS** for GAD criteria: **W**orry multiple domains, **A**lmost daily ≥6 months, **T**ension, **C**oncentration issues, **H**ours of worry, **E**asily fatigued, **R**estlessness, **S**leep disturbance

### Anxiety Treatment: First-Line Is SSRIs (Yes, Really)
**SSRIs/SNRIs are FIRST-LINE for ALL anxiety disorders** — not benzodiazepines!

**Medication Options**:
| Drug | Starting Dose | Target | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Escitalopram** | 5mg | 10-20mg | Well tolerated, also treats comorbid depression |
| **Sertraline** | 25mg | 50-200mg | Good first choice for panic + GAD |
| **Fluoxetine** | 10mg | 20-60mg | Activating — can worsen anxiety initially! |
| **Venlafaxine XR (SNRI)** | 37.5mg | 75-225mg | Effective but more side effects, BP monitoring needed |
| **Duloxetine (SNRI)** | 30mg | 60-120mg | Good if comorbid chronic pain |

**Key counseling point**: Anxiety can WORSEN in the first 1-2 weeks of SSRI. Warn patients, consider starting at half-dose, and give 'rescue' benzodiazepine for first 2 weeks if severe (limited supply, no refills).

**Benzodiazepines**: Short-term bridge ONLY (2-4 weeks). NOT first-line. Risks: tolerance, dependence, falls (esp. elderly), cognitive impairment, withdrawal.

**Buspar (Buspirone)**: Okay for GAD (not for panic). Takes 2-4 weeks. No dependency risk. Less effective than SSRIs. Useful in patients who can't tolerate SSRIs.

**Non-Pharmacologic**:
• **CBT** — gold standard psychotherapy for anxiety
• **Exposure therapy** — especially for panic disorder with agoraphobia
• **Mindfulness, relaxation techniques, exercise** — all evidence-supported
• **Limit caffeine, alcohol, stimulant use**

[Visual: Treatment algorithm: Anxiety diagnosis → SSRI initiation with half-dose first week → Limited benzo rescue if needed → CBT referral → Follow-up in 2-4 weeks]

🔥 **Benzodiazepines are NOT first-line for anxiety** — this is the #1 prescribing error in primary care for anxiety disorders. They're appropriate for: short-term (2-4 wk) bridge while SSRI kicks in, severe panic attacks (as-needed), or procedural anxiety. They're INAPPROPRIATE as monotherapy for GAD. **CBT and SSRIs are the standard of care**.

**Q:** A patient with recurrent unexpected panic attacks who now avoids driving and crowded places likely has:
  [ ] Generalized anxiety disorder
  [✓] Panic disorder with agoraphobia
  [ ] Social anxiety disorder
  [ ] Specific phobia of driving
  *Recurrent UNEXPECTED panic attacks + fear of attacks + avoidance behavior = panic disorder with agoraphobia. The avoidance is driven by fear of having another attack.*

**Q:** Which is the FIRST-LINE pharmacologic treatment for generalized anxiety disorder?
  [ ] Lorazepam PRN
  [✓] SSRI (e.g., escitalopram, sertraline)
  [ ] Buspirone monotherapy
  [ ] Propranolol as needed
  *SSRIs/SNRIs are first-line for ALL anxiety disorders. Benzodiazepines are only for short-term bridging. Buspirone is less effective and only works for GAD, not panic.*

## 🌪️ Bipolar Disorder: Don't Miss (or Make Worse)

### Why This Matters
Jake, 24, was started on fluoxetine for 'depression' 3 weeks ago. Now he's back: he's sleeping 3 hours a night, started 4 new business projects (all 'guaranteed to make millions'), calls friends at 3 AM with ideas, and spent $5,000 on 'investments.' He's grandiose, irritable, and his girlfriend is terrified. Antidepressant monotherapy in an undiagnosed bipolar patient triggered a manic episode. This is the most common way bipolar is missed — and why screening for bipolar BEFORE starting antidepressants is critical.

[Visual: Split image: 'Before antidepressant' (depressed, low energy) vs. 'After antidepressant' (manic, grandiosity, spending, insomnia)]

### Bipolar I, Bipolar II & Cyclothymia
**Bipolar I Disorder**:
• At least ONE manic episode (may have had depressive episodes)
• **Manic episode** — ≥1 week of elevated/irritable/expansive mood + ≥3 of (4 if irritable):
  - Inflated self-esteem/grandiosity
  - Decreased need for sleep (feels rested after 3 hours)
  - More talkative than usual / pressured speech
  - Flight of ideas / racing thoughts
  - Distractibility
  - Increased goal-directed activity (social, work, sexual) or psychomotor agitation
  - Excessive involvement in risky activities (spending sprees, sexual indiscretions, foolish business investments)
• **Causes marked impairment** (hospitalization, psychosis, or functional decline)

**Bipolar II Disorder**:
• At least ONE hypomanic episode + ONE major depressive episode
• **Hypomania** — same criteria as mania but:
  - Duration ≥4 days (not 7)
  - NOT severe enough to cause marked impairment or hospitalization
  - No psychotic features
• Depression is typically the DOMINANT phase (more time depressed than hypomanic)

**Cyclothymia**: Chronic fluctuation between hypomanic and depressive symptoms for ≥2 years, but never meeting full criteria for hypomanic or major depressive episode

**Mood Disorder Questionnaire (MDQ)** — Screen ALL depression before starting antidepressants:
1. 13 yes/no questions about manic/hypomanic symptoms
2. Were symptoms during same time period?
3. How severe (no-mild vs. moderate-severe problem)?
• **Positive screen**: ≥7 'yes' + same period + moderate-severe problems → refer to psychiatry

[Visual: Mood graph over time showing Bipolar I (extreme highs + lows), Bipolar II (hypomanic highs + major lows), and Cyclothymia (low-level cycling)]

**Memory Trick:** **DIG FAST** for mania: **D**istractibility, **I**nsomnia (decreased need), **G**randiosity, **F**light of ideas, **A**ctivity increase, **S**peech pressured, **T**houghtlessness (risky behavior)

🔥 **NEVER start antidepressant monotherapy without screening for bipolar first!** Screening tools (MDQ) take 5 minutes. If bipolar is suspected, refer to psychiatry. Antidepressants can trigger mania/hypomania, rapid cycling, or mixed features. If patient is already on antidepressant and develops mania → STOP the antidepressant and start mood stabilizer (lithium, valproate, or atypical antipsychotic).

### Mood Stabilizers: The Basics for Primary Care
**Lithium** — Gold standard for Bipolar I (mania prevention + suicide risk reduction):
• **Initiation**: 300mg BID or TID, titrate to level 0.6-1.2 mEq/L
• **Monitoring**: Baseline BMP, TSH, ECG (if >40), then q3-6 months
• **Side effects**: Polyuria, polydipsia, tremor, weight gain, hypothyroidism, renal impairment
• **Toxicity**: N/V, diarrhea, slurred speech, ataxia, tremor (level >1.5); can be fatal (>2.5)
• **Drug interactions**: NSAIDs, ACE-I, diuretics, thiazides INCREASE lithium levels

**Valproate (Depakote)**:
• Effective for acute mania, especially rapid cycling or mixed features
• **Monitoring**: LFTs, CBC, valproate level
• **Side effects**: Weight gain, tremor, thrombocytopenia, hepatotoxicity, PCOS-like effects
• **Teratogenic** — avoid in pregnancy

**Atypical Antipsychotics** (increasingly first-line for acute mania):
• **Olanzapine (Zyprexa)**: Most weight gain, metabolic syndrome
• **Risperidone**: Prolactin elevation, weight gain
• **Quetiapine (Seroquel)**: Sedation, weight gain — also effective for bipolar depression
• **Lurasidone (Latuda)**: Minimal metabolic effects — good for bipolar depression
• **Monitoring**: Baseline + q3mo: weight, waist circumference, fasting glucose/lipids, AIMS for tardive dyskinesia

**When to REFER**:
• First episode of mania/hypomania (needs confirmation + medication initiation)
• Mixed features (mania + depression simultaneously)
• Rapid cycling (≥4 mood episodes/year)
• Psychotic features
• Treatment resistance
• Suicide risk
• Complex comorbidity

[Visual: Three-column medication comparison: Lithium (renal/thyroid monitoring), Valproate (liver/CBC), Atypicals (metabolic monitoring) with checkmark icons for indications and warning icons for side effects]

### Pretreatment Workup Before Antidepressants
✅ **Screen for bipolar** with **MDQ** (Mood Disorder Questionnaire) — positive screen = don't start antidepressant alone
✅ **Ask about**: Personal history of manic/hypomanic episodes, family history of bipolar or suicide
✅ **If ANY suspicion**: Refer to psychiatry before starting medication
✅ **If clear unipolar depression**: Start SSRI
✅ **If starting SSRI**: Counsel about possible activation in young adults
✅ **Educate patient**: 'If you feel a sudden huge mood elevation, less need for sleep, or racing thoughts — call me immediately'
✅ **Document**: Clinical reasoning for antidepressant choice and bipolar screen results

[Visual: Safety checklist with 'GO' (green light) for unipolar depression and 'STOP' (red light) for bipolar suspicion]

**Q:** A 30-year-old with depression is started on sertraline. Two weeks later, they return euphoric, sleeping 3 hours/night, and planning a cross-country move. What happened?
  [ ] SSRI is working as expected
  [ ] This is an allergic reaction
  [✓] Antidepressant triggered a manic episode in undiagnosed bipolar
  [ ] They need a higher dose
  *Decreased need for sleep + grandiosity + increased goal-directed activity = mania. Antidepressant monotherapy can trigger a manic switch in bipolar patients. Stop the SSRI and start a mood stabilizer.*

**Q:** Which screening tool should be used BEFORE starting antidepressants to rule out bipolar disorder?
  [ ] PHQ-9
  [ ] GAD-7
  [✓] Mood Disorder Questionnaire (MDQ)
  [ ] C-SSRS
  *The MDQ screens for lifetime history of manic/hypomanic symptoms. A positive screen (≥7 + co-occurrence + functional impact) should prompt psychiatry referral before starting antidepressants.*

## 📊 Health Disparities: Why Who You Are Affects Your Health

### Why This Matters
Two patients with hypertension: Marcus, 55, Black, lives in a food desert, works two jobs with no sick leave, and is 'non-adherent' with his lisinopril. David, 55, White, retired teacher, picks up his prescription on time at the pharmacy across the street. The FNP documents Marcus as 'non-compliant.' But Marcus can't afford the meds, the pharmacy is 3 miles away and closes before his second shift ends, and the only 'grocery store' nearby is a convenience store. 'Non-adherent' isn't a character flaw — it's a systems problem. Health disparities are the #1 reason some populations live 5-10 years less than others.

[Visual: Side-by-side comparison: Same diagnosis (HTN) but different SDOH factors leading to different outcomes, showing the 'iceberg' of what's really going on]

### Social Determinants of Health & Health Equity
**Health Disparities**: Preventable differences in disease burden, outcomes, or access between populations. They are NOT about race/ethnicity itself — they reflect systems of disadvantage.

**Social Determinants of Health (SDOH)** — The conditions where people are born, live, learn, work, play, worship, and age:

1. **Economic Stability**: Employment, income, expenses, debt, medical bills, food security
2. **Education Access**: Literacy, language, vocational training, higher education
3. **Healthcare Access**: Insurance, proximity to care, transportation, health literacy
4. **Neighborhood & Built Environment**: Housing, safety, parks, walkability, food environment
5. **Social & Community Context**: Discrimination, incarceration, social support, civic participation

**Key Statistics for FNP Boards**:
• **Infant mortality**: Black infants die at 2.3× the rate of White infants
• **Maternal mortality**: Black women are 3-4× more likely to die from pregnancy-related causes
• **HTN**: Black adults have the highest prevalence globally (40%+)
• **Diabetes**: Indigenous, Hispanic, Black, and Asian populations have 1.5-2× higher rates
• **COVID-19**: Hospitalization + death rates were 2-3× higher in Black, Hispanic, and Indigenous populations

**Implicit Bias**: Unconscious attitudes/stereotypes that affect understanding, actions, and decisions. ALL clinicians have them — they're not 'bad' but must be acknowledged and mitigated.

**Structural Racism**: Systems, policies, and practices that produce and reinforce racial inequity (redlining, housing discrimination, school funding disparities, criminal justice involvement).

[Visual: Social-ecological model: Individual at center, surrounded by concentric rings (relationship, community, societal) showing levels of influence on health]

**Memory Trick:** **SDOH**: **S**ocial context, **D**o we have **O**pportunities for **H**ealth? — **5 Domains**: **E**conomic, **E**ducational, **H**ealthcare, **N**eighborhood, **C**ommunity

### Screening for SDOH in Primary Care
**PRAPARE Protocol** (Protocol for Responding to and Assessing Patient Assets, Risks, and Experiences) — CMS-recommended SDOH screening:

**Core Questions (select from these)**:
🟢 **Housing**: "Are you worried about losing your housing?" "Do you have stable housing?"
🟢 **Food**: "In the last 12 months, did you ever eat less than you felt you should because there wasn't enough money for food?"
🟢 **Utilities**: "Have you had your utilities (heat, electricity) shut off in the last year?"
🟢 **Transportation**: "Do you have a way to get to medical appointments?"
🟢 **Safety**: "Do you feel physically and emotionally safe where you live?"
🟢 **Employment**: "Are you currently employed?"
🟢 **Education**: "What is the highest level of school you completed?"
🟢 **Social Support**: "Do you have someone you can count on for help?"
🟢 **Health Literacy**: "How confident are you filling out medical forms by yourself?"

**When a need is identified**:
1. Acknowledge: 'Thank you for sharing this with me. That can be really hard.'
2. Connect: Refer to social work, community health worker, or resource specialist
3. Use resources: 2-1-1 (community services), Benefits.gov, local food banks, sliding scale clinics
4. Follow up: 'I'd like to check in about this at your next visit.'

**CMS**: Starting 2024, hospitals collect SDOH screening data. FQHCs use PRAPARE as standard.

[Visual: PRAPARE screening form with checkboxes for each SDOH domain and arrows pointing to each community resource type]

🔥 **Food insecurity affects 1 in 8 US households** — and it directly impacts chronic disease management. Someone who doesn't know where their next meal comes from CANNOT prioritize taking a daily statin. Screen all patients, not just those who 'look like' they're struggling. **The 'single-question food insecurity screen'** ('In the last 12 months, did you worry that food would run out before you had money to buy more?') has 97% sensitivity. Use it.

**Q:** Which of the following is an example of a health disparity rooted in systemic factors rather than individual behavior?
  [ ] A patient declining the flu vaccine despite education
  [✓] Black women having 3-4× higher maternal mortality than White women
  [ ] An athlete choosing not to wear a helmet
  [ ] A patient preferring herbal remedies
  *The Black maternal mortality disparity persists even when controlling for education, income, and insurance — pointing to systemic factors like implicit bias, stress from racism, and differential quality of care.*

**Q:** A clinician finds themselves spending less time with patients of certain backgrounds. What is the BEST response?
  [ ] Ignore it — it's subconscious so it doesn't matter
  [ ] Deny it — good clinicians don't have biases
  [✓] Acknowledge it as possible implicit bias and consciously address it (e.g., equal visit time, checklists)
  [ ] Only see patients from backgrounds you identify with
  *Everyone has implicit biases. Acknowledgment + active mitigation (same wait times, same visit lengths, using clinical algorithms/checklists to ensure equity) is the professional standard.*

## 🗣️ Cross-Cultural Communication: LEARN & ETHNIC Frameworks

### Why This Matters
Mai, 68, Vietnamese immigrant, nods along through the entire visit. The FNP explains her diabetes medications, diet changes, and follow-up schedule. 'Do you understand?' The patient smiles and nods. 'Any questions?' She shakes her head 'no.' The FNP feels good. But Mai understood maybe 30% and is too embarrassed or culturally deferential to say so. She'll go home, not take the medications, and her A1c will climb. This is the 'illusion of communication' — and it's dangerous. Healthcare communication across cultures is a skill, not a given.

[Visual: Two thought bubbles: What the provider thinks (clear instructions, understanding) vs. what the patient thinks (confusion, embarrassment, cultural deference)]

### The LEARN & ETHNIC Frameworks
**LEARN Model** (Cross-cultural communication framework):

**L** — **Listen**: Listen to the patient's perception of the problem. 'What do you think is causing your symptoms?' 'What does this illness mean to you?'
**E** — **Explain**: Explain your perception of the problem. 'From my perspective and the test results, here's what I think is going on.'
**A** — **Acknowledge**: Acknowledge and discuss differences/similarities between the two perspectives. 'I see that in your culture this symptom is understood differently. Let me explain what we know from a medical perspective.'
**R** — **Recommend**: Recommend a treatment plan that incorporates the patient's perspective. 'Given what you've shared, I think this treatment could work for you.'
**N** — **Negotiate**: Negotiate an agreed-upon plan. 'What do you think would work best for you? Can we try this approach for 2 weeks and see how it goes?'

**ETHNIC Framework** (More detailed, good for complex cultural scenarios):
**E** — **Explanation**: How do you explain your illness? (Patient's explanatory model)
**T** — **Treatment**: What treatments have you tried? (Home remedies, traditional, complementary)
**H** — **Healers**: Have you sought advice from traditional or alternative healers?
**N** — **Negotiate**: Negotiate mutually acceptable options
**I** — **Intervention**: Agree on an intervention that incorporates patient's beliefs
**C** — **Collaborate**: Collaborate with patient, family, and community healers

**Health Belief Model**: Patients act based on perceived: (1) susceptibility, (2) severity, (3) benefits of action, (4) barriers to action. Ask about each to understand non-adherence.

[Visual: Two hands meeting in the middle: clinician hand (biomedical model, evidence-based) and patient hand (cultural beliefs, lived experience) with LEARN model at the intersection]

**Memory Trick:** **LEARN**: **L**isten to their story, **E**xplain your view, **A**cknowledge differences, **R**ecommend together, **N**egotiate a plan

### Medical Interpretation: Professional vs. Ad Hoc
**FEDERAL REQUIREMENT**: Title VI of the Civil Rights Act requires healthcare providers receiving federal funds to provide meaningful language access. This includes medical interpretation services.

**WHEN to use an interpreter**: ANY time there is a language barrier, even if the patient seems to 'know some English.' Medical concepts are complex. Informed consent requires full understanding.

**Who should interpret?**:
| Interpreter Type | PROS | CONS | When to Use? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Professional medical interpreter** (in-person, video, phone) | Accurate, confidential, trained in medical terminology, neutral | Cost (often free — hospital pays), time | **FIRST CHOICE** always |
| **Bilingual provider/staff** | Convenient, relationship | May lack medical terminology, role confusion, may not be certified | Acceptable if certified or assessed for medical language proficiency |
| **Family member (adult)** | Patient comfort, trust | NOT confidential, may filter/edit information, emotional burden, may not translate accurately | **Last resort** — only if patient specifically refuses professional interpreter and understands risks |
| **Child (minor)** | Convenience | **NEVER acceptable** — inappropriate, breaches parent-child roles, confidentiality violation, child may filter/interpret inaccurately | **NEVER** |

**Best practices with an interpreter**:
• Address the PATIENT, not the interpreter ('What brings you in today?' NOT 'Ask him what brings him in')
• Speak in short segments, pause for interpretation
• Use plain language (no medical jargon)
• Watch the patient — watch for nonverbal cues
• Confirm understanding: 'Can you tell me in your own words what we talked about?' (teach-back)
• Document: 'Interpreted via [name/phone/video/type], language [X]'

[Visual: Triangle diagram: Clinician at one point, Patient at another, Interpreter at the third — with arrows showing ALL communication flows through the interpreter, with DOs and DON'Ts listed]

🔥 **Using a MINOR child as an interpreter is unethical and potentially illegal** — it violates professional boundaries, places emotional burden on the child, and risks inaccurate interpretation. Hospitals must provide qualified interpreters. Also: **Phone and video interpretation are just as effective as in-person** and often more available. Don't delay care waiting for in-person if phone is available.

**Q:** A 45-year-old Spanish-speaking patient in your clinic needs a complex medication discussion. The patient's 12-year-old bilingual child is with them. Best approach?
  [ ] Use the child as interpreter — it's convenient and familiar
  [✓] Use a professional medical interpreter (phone/video/in-person)
  [ ] Speak slowly and loudly in English
  [ ] Give written instructions in Spanish and send them home
  *Minors should NEVER serve as medical interpreters. Professional medical interpretation is required for complex discussions to ensure accuracy, confidentiality, and informed consent.*

**Q:** According to the LEARN model, what is the FIRST step in cross-cultural communication?
  [ ] Explain your medical opinion
  [✓] Listen to the patient's perception of their illness
  [ ] Negotiate a treatment plan
  [ ] Recommend evidence-based treatment
  *L = Listen first. Understanding the patient's explanatory model (what they think is wrong, what they've tried, what they expect) is the foundation before explaining your own perspective.*

## 🤝 Cultural Humility: From Knowledge to Action

### Why This Matters
Fatima, 34, Muslim, is expecting her first baby. Her OB tells her she'll need a C-section for breech presentation. She asks if her husband can be in the OR. The OB says no — 'hospital policy.' She asks for a female surgeon. 'Not available today.' She requests her postpartum care be done by female providers. 'We'll try but can't guarantee.' Fatima feels unheard and disrespected. She leaves against medical advice. This isn't about being 'difficult' — it's about cultural and religious needs that CAN be accommodated with effort. Cultural humility means recognizing that the PATIENT'S values matter and adapting care.

[Visual: Clinician's door sign showing multiple languages and symbols representing different cultural/religious accommodations available]

### Cultural Competence vs. Cultural Humility
**Cultural Competence** (older model):
• Learning facts about different cultures
• 'Mastering' cultural knowledge
• Risk: Stereotyping ('All [group X] believe...')
• Problem: Cultures are NOT monoliths — within-group variation is huge

**Cultural Humility** (current standard):
• Lifelong commitment to self-evaluation and self-critique
• Recognizing power imbalances
• Building respectful partnerships with patients
• ASKING rather than ASSUMING: 'Tell me what's important to you about your health care.'
• Three principles:
  1. **Life-long learning** — you'll never 'arrive'
  2. **Acknowledge power imbalances** — provider has institutional power
  3. **Institutional accountability** — organizations must support equity

**CLAS Standards** (National Standards for Culturally and Linguistically Appropriate Services):
• Provide culturally competent care
• Offer language access services
• Support organizational accountability for cultural competence

**Key shifts**:
• FROM 'learning about a culture' TO 'learning from each patient'
• FROM 'treating everyone the same' (equality) TO 'giving everyone what they need' (equity)
• FROM 'blaming the patient' TO 'examining the system'

[Visual: Illustration: Two people standing on boxes to see over a fence — one on stacked boxes (equity vs. equality metaphor), with 'Cultural Humility' bridging the gap]

**Memory Trick:** **ASK** for cultural humility: **A**ssume nothing, **S**eek to understand, **K**nowledge is co-created with each patient

### Integrating Cultural Needs Into Clinical Care
**Religious/Cultural Accommodations in Healthcare**:

| Need | Accommodation |
|---|---|
| **Prayer times** | Ask about timing, plan procedures around them, provide private space |
| **Modesty/sex of provider** | Ask about preference, document it, honor it when possible |
| **Dietary restrictions** | Hospital meals, contrast dyes, gelatin in meds, liquid med contents (alcohol/gelatin) |
| **Fasting (Ramadan)** | Adjust medication timing (once vs. twice daily), discuss whether exemptions apply |
| **Blood products (Jehovah's Witness)** | Explore alternatives: iron, EPO, cell salvage, volume expanders |
| **End-of-life beliefs** | Advance directives vary by culture. Some cultures prioritize family decision-making over individual autonomy. ASK. |
| **Traditional remedies** | Ask non-judgmentally: 'Are you taking any herbs, teas, or traditional medicines?' Many interact with prescriptions |
| **Physical exam** | Explain what you're doing and why before touching. Offer chaperone for ALL genders. |

**Respecting Traditional Medicine**:
• Many patients use traditional healers, herbs, acupuncture, Ayurveda, TCM alongside Western medicine
• Ask OPENLY: 'Some of my patients use herbs or traditional treatments. Have you tried anything like that?'
• Labeling it 'unproven' or dismissive damages trust
• Learn about common interactions (e.g., St. John's Wort lowers SSRI efficacy, ginkgo increases bleeding risk)

**Shared Decision-Making Across Cultures**:
• Some cultures prioritize FAMILY decisions over individual autonomy ('I can't decide without my husband/son/family council')
• Ask: 'Who do you want involved in these decisions?'
• Document the decision-making structure
• Respect it — it's not 'non-adherence,' it's cultural

[Visual: Table/grid showing common cultural/religious accommodation requests on the left and specific actionable responses on the right]

🔥 **Don't assume a patient's beliefs based on appearance, name, or background** — a patient who looks Muslim may be atheist, a patient with a Mexican surname may not speak Spanish, a patient who 'looks Asian' may be third-generation American. Cultural humility means **ask, don't assume**. And **The 'golden rule' becomes the 'platinum rule'**: Treat others the way THEY want to be treated (not the way you want to be treated).

**Q:** A patient who appears to be from a specific ethnic background refuses certain medical interventions. What is the BEST approach?
  [ ] Assume it's a cultural belief and document as such
  [✓] Ask the patient directly: 'What's important to you about this decision? Is there anything about your background or beliefs I should understand?'
  [ ] Document as non-adherent and refer to social work
  [ ] Insist on the intervention — it's evidence-based
  *Cultural humility means asking the patient rather than assuming. The refusal could be cultural, religious, experiential, or preference-based. Only the patient can tell you.*

**Q:** Cultural humility differs from cultural competence in that cultural humility emphasizes:
  [ ] Mastering a body of knowledge about different cultures
  [ ] Having interpreters available for all languages
  [✓] A lifelong commitment to self-reflection, acknowledging power imbalances, and learning from each patient
  [ ] Following standardized protocols for diverse populations
  *Cultural humility is about lifelong learning, self-critique, and recognizing power imbalances — rather than 'mastering' cultural facts (which can lead to stereotyping). Both are needed for excellent care.*
